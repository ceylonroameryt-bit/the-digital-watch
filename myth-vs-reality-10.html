Myth: "Change Your Password Every 90 Days"

"Your password expires in 3 days. Please choose a new password." For years, that message was a routine part of working life. Summer2024! became Autumn2024!, then Winter2024!. It felt like security. In reality, it mostly made passwords weaker, and the organisations that write the rules have said so.

The myth "Changing your password regularly, every 60 or 90 days, keeps your account secure. The more often, the better."

Where the Rule Came From

The idea made sense on paper. If an attacker steals a password, forcing a change limits how long they can use it. If they are slowly cracking a stolen password database, changing passwords regularly means they finish too late.

Microsoft explained the logic, and its flaw, when it removed password expiry from its Windows security baselines in 2019. Periodic expiry, it wrote, is a defence only against a password being stolen during its validity period and then used. If a password is never stolen, there is no need to expire it. And if you have evidence it has been stolen, you would act immediately rather than wait for it to expire.

Microsoft also pointed out how long the window is. The Windows default was 42 days, and its own baseline had said 60, and before that 90. That is a very long time to let a thief keep using a stolen password.

What Forced Changes Actually Do

The real problem is human. People forced to invent a new password every few months do not invent a strong, unrelated one each time. Microsoft summarised it: when people are forced to change their passwords, too often they make a small and predictable alteration to their existing password, or forget the new one.

The UK National Cyber Security Centre made the same case in 2016. Its guidance noted that the new password is likely to be similar to the old one, is more likely to be written down, and may be reused elsewhere, and concluded that the more often users are forced to change passwords, the greater the overall vulnerability to attack.

Research backs this up. In a 2010 study of more than 7,700 accounts, researchers at the University of North Carolina examined how people choose replacements for expired passwords and built tools to guess the new password from the old one. They concluded that their findings call into question the merit of continuing the practice of password expiration.

Forced expiry did not produce new passwords. It produced old passwords with a different number on the end.

What the Rules Say Now

  • NIST. The current US digital identity guidelines, SP 800-63B-4, say organisations must not require users to change passwords periodically, but must force a change if there is evidence of compromise. They also require new passwords to be checked against lists of common and breached passwords, and allow password managers.
  • NCSC. The UK guidance advises organisations not to force regular expiry, and to invest instead in monitoring that helps spot unauthorised access, such as showing users their last login.
  • Microsoft. Its 2019 baseline called periodic password expiration an "ancient and obsolete mitigation of very low value", and recommended banned-password lists and multi-factor authentication instead.

Some industry standards and auditors still ask for regular changes, so you may still see expiry policies at work. But the direction from the bodies that set best practice is clear.

When You Should Change a Password

"Don't change on a schedule" does not mean "never change". Change a password immediately when:

  • A service tells you it has been breached, or a breach alert service shows your email in a new leak.
  • You notice sign-ins, emails or activity you do not recognise.
  • You typed it into a site you now think was fake.
  • You have used it on more than one account. Give each account its own.
  • You shared it with someone who should no longer have access.
  • Your device may have been infected with malware.

After a breach, follow the steps in Security in 5 Minutes, Episode 09, and if it is an organisation's account, Playbook Monday, Episode 02.

Verdict: Myth Changing passwords on a fixed schedule leads to predictable, weaker passwords and gives little real protection. NIST, the NCSC and Microsoft advise against it. Use long, unique passwords, add multi-factor authentication or passkeys, and change a password when there is a reason to.

Season One in Review

Over ten episodes, we have looked at some of the most widely believed ideas in everyday security:

  • Incognito mode hides your browsing from people who share your device, not from the internet.
  • Macs and phones do get malware, usually by persuading the user to let it in.
  • A strong password stops guessing, not phishing, reuse or theft.
  • Public Wi-Fi is far less dangerous than it was, thanks to HTTPS, but not risk-free.
  • Nobody is too small to be a target when attacks are automated.
  • A VPN moves your trust; it does not make you secure.
  • Antivirus is one layer, not a complete defence.
  • The padlock means an encrypted connection, not an honest site.
  • Deleting a file often leaves it recoverable.
  • And forced password changes made passwords weaker, not stronger.

The common thread is that real security rarely comes from a single tool, icon or rule. It comes from a few good habits, applied consistently: unique passwords in a password manager, multi-factor authentication or passkeys, automatic updates, backups, and a healthy suspicion of anything that asks you to hurry.

Final Thoughts: The End of Myth vs Reality, Season One

Security advice changes as attacks change, and some of yesterday's rules have become today's myths. The 90-day password change is the clearest example: once standard everywhere, now advised against by the organisations that wrote the rules. Keep questioning what you were told, check who is saying it and why, and update your habits when the evidence moves.

Good security advice has an expiry date. Your password does not need one.
