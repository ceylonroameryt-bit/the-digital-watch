Account Takeover Response: A Checklist That Closes Every Back Door

PS
Poorna Sujampathi Rathnayaka
Writer · Cyber Insight · Playbook Monday

An employee's account has been used to send phishing emails to half your customers. The password has been changed. The attacker is still in. This is the most common mistake in account takeover response: treating the password as the only door, when attackers always leave themselves a few more.

Once an attacker has an account, they work quickly to keep it. They add their own MFA method, create forwarding rules, grant access to a malicious app, or keep a signed-in session open. A password reset on its own closes none of these.

This playbook follows Microsoft's guidance on responding to a compromised Microsoft 365 account, with the reasoning behind each step. If you use Google Workspace or another platform, the same doors exist under different names.

Changing the password locks the front door. Account takeover response means checking every window as well.
At a glance 1. Disable the account (or reset the password)
2. Revoke all sessions and refresh tokens
3. Remove attacker MFA methods and devices
4. Remove malicious app consents
5. Remove unexpected admin roles
6. Remove forwarding and malicious inbox rules
7. Investigate what the attacker did
8. Restore, communicate and harden

How You Usually Find Out

Microsoft lists these common signs of a compromised mailbox:

  • The mailbox is blocked from sending email, usually after sending spam.
  • Missing or deleted email.
  • Inbox rules that forward mail to unknown addresses, or move messages into Notes, Junk Email or RSS Subscriptions.
  • Strange messages in Sent Items or Deleted Items.
  • Unexpected changes to the user's details, signature or external forwarding.
  • Frequent password changes or unexplained lockouts.

Step 1: Disable the Account

Microsoft's preferred first step is to disable the account entirely until the investigation is finished. With Microsoft Graph PowerShell: 

Connect-MgGraph -Scopes "User.ReadWrite.All"
$user = Get-MgUser -Search UserPrincipalName:'user@example.com' -ConsistencyLevel Eventual
Update-MgUser -UserId $user.Id -AccountEnabled $false

If disabling is not possible, reset the password instead, and follow Microsoft's cautions:

  • Never send the new password by email. The attacker may still be reading the mailbox.
  • If the account is synced from on-premises Active Directory, reset the password there, and reset it twice to reduce the risk of pass-the-hash attacks.
  • Delete and recreate app passwords. Microsoft points out they are not revoked by a password reset.

Step 2: Revoke Every Session

This is the step most often missed. If the attacker stole a session cookie or refresh token, they stay signed in whatever the password is. Microsoft says revoking sessions immediately invalidates active access using the stolen credentials: 

Connect-MgGraph -Scopes User.RevokeSessions.All
Revoke-MgUserSignInSession -UserId user@example.com

Steps 3 to 5: Remove the Attacker's Footholds

Step 3: MFA methods and devices. Review every MFA method and registered device on the account. Remove anything the user does not recognise. Attackers often add their own authenticator app or phone number so they can sign in again later.

Step 4: App consents. Review the applications the user has granted access to, and remove and revoke any that should not be there. A malicious app keeps access even after the password changes (see Threat of the Week, Episode 02).

Step 5: Admin roles. Check whether any administrative roles have been assigned to the user, in Entra ID, Azure and the security portals, and remove any that should not be there.

Step 6: Forwarding and Inbox Rules

Check mailbox-level forwarding, then every inbox rule, including hidden ones: 

Get-Mailbox -Identity user@example.com | Format-List Forwarding*Address,DeliverTo*
Get-InboxRule -Mailbox user@example.com -IncludeHidden | Format-List Name,Enabled,RedirectTo,Forward*,Identity

A value in ForwardingSmtpAddress means mail is going to an external address. In the rules, look at RedirectTo, ForwardTo and ForwardAsAttachmentTo, and at any rule that deletes or moves messages. Remove anything the user did not create.

Step 7: Find Out What the Attacker Did

  • Sign-in logs. Microsoft suggests examining IP addresses, locations, times and success or failure, to establish when the attacker first got in.
  • Audit logs. Search from just before the suspicious activity began, without filtering by activity type at first, so nothing is missed.
  • Sent mail. Use message trace and the Sent Items folder to see who the attacker emailed. These people need warning.
  • Files. Review OneDrive and SharePoint activity for downloads or sharing links created during the compromise.
  • How they got in. Phishing, password reuse, an infostealer on a personal device or MFA fatigue. The answer decides what you fix next.

Step 8: Restore, Communicate, Harden

  • Re-enable the account with a new password, delivered to the user in person or by phone.
  • Unblock sending if the mailbox was restricted for spam. Microsoft has a specific process for removing users from the Restricted entities list.
  • Warn recipients of any malicious emails sent from the account, especially customers and suppliers.
  • Require phishing-resistant MFA for the account. Microsoft strongly recommends MFA for every account, and phishing-resistant MFA for admins.
  • Consider whether it is a reportable breach. If personal data was accessed, UK organisations may need to report to the ICO within 72 hours.

The Short Version for Personal Accounts

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre gives this order for recovering a hacked personal account:

  1. Use the provider's official recovery process from its website.
  2. Check your email for forwarding rules.
  3. Change the password, and the password on any account that used the same one.
  4. Sign out of all devices and apps.
  5. Turn on two-step verification.
  6. Update your devices.
  7. Tell your contacts not to trust recent messages from you.
  8. Check bank and shopping accounts, and report any fraud.

For Google and Microsoft personal accounts, Security in 5 Minutes, Episode 06 shows where each of these settings lives.

Final Thoughts

Account takeover response is a checklist exercise, and that is its strength. Attackers use the same handful of persistence tricks again and again: their own MFA method, a live session, a forwarding rule, an app consent, an admin role. Close all of them, every time, and the attacker has nowhere left to stand.

Disable, revoke, remove, investigate, restore. Skip a step and you may be doing it all again next week.
