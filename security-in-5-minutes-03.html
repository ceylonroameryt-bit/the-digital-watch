Password Managers: How to Choose One and Move Your Passwords Across Safely

You already know you should not reuse passwords. The trouble is that nobody can remember eighty different ones. A password manager solves that problem, as long as you pick one sensibly and set it up properly.

Most people have a small set of passwords they rotate between. One for email, a slightly different one for shopping sites, the same one with a number on the end for work. It feels manageable, and it is exactly the habit criminals rely on.

When a website is breached and its passwords leak, attackers do not stop at that website. They take each email address and password pair and try it on email providers, banks, shops and social networks. This is called credential stuffing, and it works because so many people reuse passwords.

Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found that using stolen login details was the most common way attackers got into organisations, at 22% of breaches. The same thing happens to personal accounts every day.

The goal is not to remember stronger passwords. It is to stop needing to remember them at all.

1. What a Password Manager Actually Does

A password manager is an encrypted vault for your logins. It creates a long, random password for each site, stores it, and fills it in when you visit that site. You remember one strong password, or unlock it with your fingerprint or face, and it remembers everything else.

It also helps in a way most people do not expect. Because it fills in passwords based on the website's address, it will not offer your bank password on a fake bank page. If the password does not appear when you expect it to, that is a warning sign worth paying attention to.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) recommends using one, because it is the practical way to have a different password for every account.

2. Built-In or Standalone: Which Should You Choose?

You have two broad options, and both are reasonable.

The one built into your phone or browser. Apple devices have the Passwords app and iCloud Keychain. Android and Chrome use Google Password Manager. Microsoft Edge and Firefox have their own. They are free, already installed and very easy to use.

The NCSC says it is safe to save passwords in your browser on your own devices. It also points out the catch: those passwords are only as secure as the device and account they are stored in. Keep your browser and operating system updated, lock your devices with a PIN, password or biometric, and never save passwords on a shared or public computer.

A standalone password manager. Apps such as Bitwarden, 1Password, Proton Pass and others work across every browser and operating system. They usually add features such as secure notes, sharing passwords with family members, and alerts when a saved password appears in a known breach.

Choose the built-in option if… You mostly stay within one ecosystem (all Apple, or Android and Chrome), and convenience matters most.
Choose a standalone app if… You mix devices (an iPhone with a Windows laptop, for example), want to share passwords with family, or do not want to be tied to one company.

Whichever you choose, the NCSC's advice is the same: the best password manager is the one that fits how you actually use your devices. A good manager you use every day beats a perfect one you abandon after a week.

3. What the LastPass Breach Taught Us

A fair question is whether putting every password in one place is a risk in itself. The best real-world answer comes from LastPass.

In December 2022, LastPass disclosed that an attacker had copied a backup of customer vault data. The usernames, passwords, secure notes and form data in those vaults were encrypted with 256-bit AES, using a key derived from each customer's master password, which LastPass says it never stores. Website addresses, however, were not encrypted.

In other words, the attackers did not get readable passwords. They got locked boxes, and the only way to open each one was to guess that customer's master password. LastPass said customers using its default settings, including a minimum of twelve characters, faced a very difficult guessing task. People with short or reused master passwords were in a much weaker position.

The lesson A password manager concentrates your risk into one master password. That makes the master password, and the account protecting it, the most important thing you set up. It does not make a password manager riskier than reusing the same few passwords everywhere.

4. Set It Up Properly

  1. Create a strong master password you have never used anywhere else. The NCSC suggests three random words, which are long but easy to remember. Make it longer than you think you need.
  2. Turn on two-step verification for the password manager account. The NCSC recommends this so that even someone who learns your master password cannot get in. Use a passkey or authenticator app where possible (see Episode 01).
  3. Save your recovery details. Most managers give you a recovery code or emergency kit. Print it or store it somewhere safe and offline. If you lose the master password without it, the company usually cannot recover your vault.
  4. Install it everywhere you sign in: the browser extension on your computer and the app on your phone, and turn on autofill.

5. Moving Your Passwords Across Safely

If your passwords currently live in your browser or another manager, you can usually move them in a few minutes. Many apps can import directly. If not, you export a file from the old one and import it into the new one.

That exported file is the part to be careful with. It is normally a plain CSV spreadsheet, and every password inside it is unencrypted. Bitwarden's own guidance is to delete the export once the import is complete.

  • Export and import in one sitting, on your own computer, not a shared one.
  • Do not email the file to yourself or save it to a cloud folder.
  • Delete it straight afterwards, and empty the recycle bin or trash.
  • Check the import worked by signing in to two or three sites using the new manager before you remove anything from the old one.
  • Then turn off password saving in the old browser or app, so you do not end up with two half-updated vaults.

6. Now Fix the Reused Passwords

Importing your old passwords is only half the job. Most managers include a security check or "password health" report that flags reused, weak and breached passwords.

Do not try to fix all of them at once. Start with the accounts that matter most: email, your Apple, Google or Microsoft account, banking, and anything that stores payment details. For each one, use the manager to generate a new random password, and where the site supports passkeys, add one instead.

Fixing five accounts a week will clear most people's list within a couple of months.

Final Thoughts

A password manager does not make you invincible. It removes the single most common weakness, which is the same password on many sites, and it makes the safe option the easy one.

Pick one tonight, protect it with a strong master password and two-step verification, move your passwords across carefully, and delete the export file. Next weekend, change the passwords on your five most important accounts. That is the whole plan.

One strong master password, protected by two-step verification, is far safer than ten weak ones you have to remember.
