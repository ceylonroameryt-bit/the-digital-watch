Turn On Automatic Updates Everywhere (and Check They Are Actually Working)

That "Update available" message you keep dismissing is usually a security fix. Sometimes it closes a hole that criminals are already using. Turning on automatic updates is one of the most effective things you can do, and it takes a few minutes per device.

On Friday 12 May 2017, a piece of ransomware called WannaCry spread across the world. In England it hit at least 81 of the 236 NHS hospital trusts, along with nearly 600 GP practices. NHS England later estimated that over 19,000 appointments were cancelled.

The National Audit Office investigated. It found that the infected organisations had been running unpatched or unsupported versions of Windows, and its head, Sir Amyas Morse, said the attack could have been prevented by following basic IT security best practice. A fix for the weakness WannaCry used had been released months before the attack.

Your devices at home face the same basic risk on a smaller scale. When a company fixes a security flaw, the update tells the world the flaw exists. Criminals then race to use it against everyone who has not updated yet.

An update you postpone is a known weakness you have chosen to keep.

1. Why Updates Matter So Much

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) describes applying updates as one of the most important, and quickest, things you can do to stay safe online. Its advice is simple: install updates as soon as you are prompted, and turn on automatic updates so you do not have to remember.

Updates are not only about new features. Most of them include security fixes for problems that could let someone run malicious code, steal data or take control of a device. Many attacks do not need you to do anything wrong at all. They only need a device that has not been updated.

2. Switch On Automatic Updates, Device by Device

iPhone and iPad: Settings, then General, then Software Update, then Automatic Updates. Turn on both downloading and installing. For apps, open Settings, then App Store, and turn on App Updates.

Android: Settings, then System or Software update (names vary by manufacturer). Also check Security update or Google Play system update. For apps, open the Play Store, tap your profile picture, then Settings, Network preferences, and Auto-update apps.

Windows: Settings, then Windows Update. Updates install automatically by default, but you can turn on "Get the latest updates as soon as they're available" and check Advanced options to make sure updates for other Microsoft products are included.

Mac: System Settings, then General, then Software Update, then the information button next to Automatic updates. Turn everything on, including "Install Security Responses and system files".

Web browsers: Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari update themselves, but usually only finish when you restart them. If your browser shows an update button or coloured icon, click it.

Router and smart devices: Check the app or settings for an automatic update option and turn it on. We covered routers in detail in Episode 02.

3. Why Automatic Updates Quietly Fail

Turning on automatic updates is not quite the end of the job. The NCSC notes that automatic updates can fail, and recommends checking occasionally that devices are keeping themselves up to date. The usual reasons are:

  • The device is never restarted. Many updates only finish installing after a restart. A laptop that is always put to sleep rather than shut down can sit on a half-installed update for weeks.
  • Storage is full. Phones in particular cannot download an update if there is no space for it.
  • It is never on Wi-Fi or charging. Many phones only install updates overnight, on Wi-Fi and while charging.
  • An update failed once and stopped trying. You may see an error in the update settings that nobody has looked at.
The monthly two-minute check Once a month, open the update settings on each of your main devices and look for "Your device is up to date" or similar, with a recent date. Restart anything that says a restart is pending. That is all.

4. When a Device Stops Getting Updates

Every device eventually reaches the end of its support. After that, new security flaws will never be fixed. The NCSC advises avoiding unsupported phones and devices where possible.

The biggest current example is Windows 10. Microsoft ended support on 14 October 2025. Windows 10 PCs still work, but Microsoft says they no longer receive security updates unless they are enrolled in its consumer Extended Security Updates programme, which can cover a Windows 10 device until 12 October 2027. Your options are:

  • Upgrade to Windows 11, which is free if your PC meets the requirements. Check in Settings, then Windows Update.
  • Enrol in Extended Security Updates to buy time, if your PC cannot be upgraded yet.
  • Replace the PC before the extended updates run out.

For phones, check how long your manufacturer promises updates when you buy one. For smart devices sold in the UK since 29 April 2024, manufacturers must publish a minimum period for security updates, so you can compare before you buy.

5. One Warning: Fake Update Messages

Criminals know people trust update messages. A pop-up on a website saying your browser or "Flash Player" is out of date, with a download button, is a common way of spreading malware.

Genuine updates come from your device's own settings, the official app store or the program itself. Never install an "update" offered by a website, an email or a pop-up advert. If you are unsure, close the page and check the update settings directly.

Final Thoughts

WannaCry is remembered as a major cyber attack, but the official conclusion was that it relied on systems that had not been kept up to date. The same lesson applies to your phone, your laptop and the router under your TV.

Spend ten minutes today switching on automatic updates on every device you own. Then add a monthly reminder to check they are working and to restart anything that has been asleep for weeks.

Turn updates on, restart now and then, and replace devices that no longer receive them.
