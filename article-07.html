The Fake Recruiter in Your LinkedIn Inbox

Your phone buzzes.

New LinkedIn message.

in
Talent Acquisition Lead
Specialist Technology & Cyber Recruitment · 500+ connections
“Hi, I came across your profile and think your experience could be a great fit for an opportunity I’m working on. Are you currently open to new roles?”

What would you do?

You’d probably open their profile.

Professional photo. Recruiter title. Company name. Hundreds of connections. A few posts about jobs they’re hiring for.

Everything looks normal.

Then you notice they’ve actually looked at your background.

They mention your experience. Maybe your current role, a certification, a project from your portfolio, or a technology you work with.

So you reply.

Why wouldn’t you?

That’s how genuine recruiters contact people every day.

But there’s one question we don’t normally ask this early:

Who am I actually talking to?

I Received a Message Like This Myself

Recently, I received a recruitment email from someone who said they had found my public portfolio.

They mentioned specific areas from my background, including SOC analysis, incident response and threat detection.

They introduced themselves as the founder of an independent recruitment consultancy and asked for my latest CV, the roles I was interested in, my location, working preferences, salary expectations, availability and sponsorship requirements.

They also said they wouldn’t send my CV to an employer without my permission.

Would you reply?

A lot of people probably would.

Because nothing about that message automatically makes it suspicious.

And to be clear, I’m not saying the person who contacted me was a fake recruiter. There isn’t enough information in that email alone to make that conclusion.

That’s actually why it’s such a useful example.

A genuine recruiter could send that exact kind of message.

A fake recruiter could send something very similar.

So instead of immediately asking:

Traditional Question: “Is this message a scam?”

There is a far more effective security posture to take:

The Verification Question: “Can I independently verify who sent it?”

Looking Professional Isn't the Same as Being Genuine

Think about how much information we put online.

  • LinkedIn might show your complete employment history, university education, certifications, and technical skills.
  • Your portfolio showcases specific architecture designs, threat analyses, and active project write-ups.
  • GitHub reveals the languages, frameworks, packages, and scripting utilities you execute every day.
  • Your feed posts might even announce that you’re actively exploring new career opportunities or open to remote work.

So imagine someone messages you:

“I noticed your experience in SOC operations, SIEM rules, and incident response.”

That feels personal. It feels tailored.

But the information was already completely public.

That doesn’t make the message suspicious by itself. Real recruiters research candidates too. They search keywords and look for specific domain expertise.

It simply highlights an essential truth of modern digital literacy:

Personalisation isn’t verification.

The same applies to the recruiter’s profile:

A professional headshot helps. A long employment history helps. Hundreds of mutual industry connections help.

But none of those things alone prove who is actually controlling the account. Compromised accounts, AI-generated headshots, and purchased or aged LinkedIn profiles are widely traded across underground markets to give fraudulent personas instant credibility.

Now Imagine You Reply

The conversation continues.

They ask about your experience.

They explain the position.

The salary sounds realistic — neither suspiciously high nor disappointing.

The location makes sense.

The job genuinely matches your background.

Then:

“I’ll send you the full job description and role specifications.”

A few minutes later:

PDF
SOC_Analyst_Job_Description.pdf
Attachment · 1.4 MB · Ready to open

Would you open it?

Almost certainly.

If a completely random stranger emailed you an unexpected attachment out of nowhere, you might hesitate.

But now it isn’t unexpected.

You’ve already spoken to the recruiter. You’ve discussed the position. You’re waiting for the document. Opening it feels completely normal.

And that’s where social engineering becomes devastatingly effective:

The file hasn’t necessarily become safer. You’ve simply become more comfortable with the person who sent it.

The Conversation Doesn't Need to Feel Suspicious

A fake recruiter doesn’t have to rush.

They can spend days asking completely normal screening questions:

  • “What is your current notice period?”
  • “What salary bracket are you targeting?”
  • “When could you earliest start?”
  • “Would you consider a hybrid working pattern?”

Then maybe:

“Can we continue on WhatsApp or schedule a quick screening call?”

Does moving to WhatsApp automatically mean it’s a scam?

No. Real recruiters use WhatsApp, Teams, corporate email, and direct phone calls every single day.

But as the conversation moves forward across channels, independent verification becomes critical.

Threat intelligence research documents extensive recruitment-themed campaigns where threat actors created detailed recruiter personas, approached targets through LinkedIn, and sustained conversations across channels such as WhatsApp and phone calls for days before eventually introducing malicious recruitment material [1].

Sometimes the attack doesn’t begin with a suspicious link.

It begins with a normal, polite conversation.

Then Comes the Recruitment Process

Maybe you have an introductory interview.

Afterwards, you receive a portal link:

“Please complete your candidate profile before the next technical stage.”

You open the link:

🔒 HTTPS Valid · Secure Candidate Application Gateway
Company logo in high resolution. Detailed job description. Structured web form. Everything looks impeccably professional.

Then the form asks for your information:

  • Your full legal name
  • Home residential address
  • Phone number and date of birth
  • Prior employment and reference history
  • Later: proof of identity (passport or driver's license scans)
  • Eventually: bank account details for payroll setup

And this is where things become difficult.

Because real employers need some of this information too.

An employer will eventually need right-to-work documents. They’ll need your details for payroll. They’ll need your bank details if they’re going to pay your salary.

So the existence of those questions doesn’t automatically make something fraudulent.

The better questions are:

1. Who am I giving this to?
Have you confirmed the recruiter's identity through an official corporate directory outside LinkedIn?
2. Why do they need it now?
No legitimate organization requires your bank routing number or passport scan before an initial interview.
3. Is the portal verified?
Is this an enterprise HR system (Workday, Greenhouse) on the company's official domain, or an unverified standalone web page?

There’s a huge difference between providing banking information through a verified employer’s HR portal after accepting an official, written contract, and typing it into a web page sent by someone you met through an unsolicited LinkedIn chat.

Sometimes They Want Your Money

One common variant of recruitment fraud is straightforward financial theft.

You complete the screening. Everything feels promising. Then you receive the message you’ve been hoping for:

“Congratulations. We’d like to offer you the position.”

It’s a remote role. The hiring manager explains that you’ll need a standardized home office setup: an approved laptop, dual monitors, a dedicated headset, and specialized software licenses.

Then comes the unusual part:

⚠️ The Financial Pivot:

“We will send you a company check to cover equipment costs. Please deposit it and purchase your equipment immediately through our approved vendor supplier portal. You will be fully reimbursed.”

That’s the moment you must stop.

A job is supposed to pay you. You should never have to send money, wire funds, or purchase supplies from an unverified "vendor" to secure employment.

Under banking regulations, deposited checks often appear in your account balance within 24 to 48 hours as "funds available." But it can take several weeks for the issuing bank to flag the check as fraudulent. When the check eventually bounces, your bank reverses the full deposit — but the money you transferred to the fake "vendor" is gone permanently.

The fake job creates enough trust and emotional investment that a financial transfer starts to feel like part of routine onboarding.

But money isn’t always what the attacker wants.

Sometimes They Want Your Login

Security briefings describe another sophisticated recruitment-themed campaign documented as RecruitTrap [2].

Attackers engineered high-fidelity lookalike recruitment portals impersonating well-known multinational brands and tech firms.

According to the threat briefing, when victims attempted to register or submit application materials, the portal actively rejected personal webmail domains such as @gmail.com or @yahoo.com, prompting:

"Personal email addresses not permitted. Please authenticate using your corporate business identity (Microsoft 365 / Google Workspace)."

Notice how that changes the adversary's objective.

The attacker isn't interested in your personal Gmail inbox.

They are hunting for corporate identity credentials, enterprise Single Sign-On (SSO) tokens, or OAuth authorization consent [2] [9].

Think about the implications:

You believe you are signing in to track an exciting prospective job application.

In reality, the attacker is harvesting credentials to breach the corporate enterprise network where you currently work.

Sometimes the “Technical Assessment” Is the Attack

This attack vector is especially dangerous if you work in software engineering, cybersecurity, data science, or IT administration.

Imagine you have completed an initial video or phone screening.

Everything went smoothly. The conversation felt legitimate.

Then:

“Our engineering team would like you to complete a brief technical assessment before scheduling the final round.”

They deliver a file:

ZIP
Technical_Assessment.zip
Password: JobInterview2026! · Coding Project & Test Suite

Or perhaps they send you a link to a GitHub repository and ask you to clone the repo, run npm install or pip install -r requirements.txt, and fix a few failing unit tests.

Would that feel suspicious?

Probably not.

Technical assessments are a universal standard in technology hiring. Software engineers run code. Cybersecurity analysts execute scripts. IT engineers test utilities.

So the attacker doesn’t need to find a remote zero-day exploit to bypass your computer’s defenses.

They don’t have to secretly execute code on your computer. They convince YOU to execute it for them.

You believe you’re demonstrating your technical expertise.

The adversary is executing an infostealer or remote access Trojan directly on your endpoint.

And this is not a theoretical exercise.

A Real-World Example: Operation Dream Job

One of the clearest real-world examples is an ongoing, high-profile espionage campaign known across the cybersecurity community as Operation Dream Job, attributed by security agencies and threat intelligence firms to the Lazarus Group / HIDDEN COBRA [3].

The campaign specifically targets professionals working in defense, aerospace, defense contracting, government, and critical technology sectors across North America and Europe [3].

What makes the operation so compelling is how completely ordinary the beginning appears.

Attackers fabricated realistic recruiter personas impersonating talent acquisition staff from premier aerospace and defense giants, including Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, and BAE Systems [4].

Imagine being the target.

You already have a full-time position. Suddenly, someone claiming to represent an industry leader reaches out privately with a highly tailored opportunity.

They’ve examined your technical background. They understand your specialized domain. The opportunity represents significant prestige and higher compensation.

So you engage in conversation.

Not for five minutes. Over days and weeks, the attackers maintain multi-channel communications across LinkedIn, WhatsApp, and scheduled phone calls, slowly cultivating trust and presenting an enticing career move [5].

Eventually, the technical materials arrive: password-protected ZIP archives containing weaponized documents, backdoored Node.js projects, or trojanized PDF viewers designed to bypass gateway email scanners and establish persistent command-and-control access [6].

Now look at the exact same situation from two very different perspectives:

👤 The Employee's Perspective

“I’m privately speaking with a recruiter about an exciting job opportunity. I’m completing a standard technical assessment to prove my capabilities.”

🎯 The Attacker's Perspective

“This person already holds high-privilege access and security clearance inside the aerospace defense contractor we want to infiltrate.”

That is what makes recruitment fraud such a potent national security and enterprise threat.

Sometimes the fake job isn’t the ultimate target.

You might simply be the route to the organization behind you.

So How Do You Know Whether a Recruiter Is Real?

There is no single silver bullet or magic indicator.

A recruiter contacting you on WhatsApp isn’t automatically fake. Someone reaching out unexpectedly isn’t automatically malicious. Being asked for your CV isn’t suspicious. And someone referencing your career achievements doesn’t prove they are genuine.

Instead of relying on appearances, verify the pieces independently:

  • Search for the recruiter independently: Search their name and agency on Google and corporate registries. Does their tenure and activity align? Are there duplicate profiles using different names with the same profile photo?
  • Inspect the actual email domain: A genuine recruiter from Lockheed Martin will email from @lmco.com or @lockheedmartin.com — never from @lockheedmartin-careers.com, @consultant.com, or a free webmail address.
  • Cross-reference the open requisition: If a recruiter pitches a specific job opening, visit the employer’s official public careers website directly and verify that the Job Requisition ID genuinely exists.
  • Escalate verification as sensitivity escalates: Discussing your general skills, career goals, and availability is standard. But if the conversation pivots toward downloading archives, running scripts, providing passport scans, entering enterprise credentials, or wiring money: STOP AND VERIFY OUT-OF-BAND.

Defensive frameworks highlight out-of-band identity verification as the core safeguard: confirming an individual’s identity through an independent, trusted communication channel rather than relying on the very conversation you are trying to authenticate [7].

Don’t ask the potentially fake recruiter to prove that the potentially fake recruiter is real. Verify them somewhere else.

Back to That First Message

Remember where we started?

“Hi, I came across your profile and think your experience could be a great fit for an opportunity.”

Would I reply?

Possibly. Because it could be completely genuine.

The takeaway here is not to distrust every recruitment inquiry or assume every recruiter is malicious. Real recruiters reach out unexpectedly every day to connect talent with opportunities.

The lesson is that contact and verification are entirely different things.

  • A polished profile is not proof.
  • A personalized message is not proof.
  • A corporate logo on a web form is not proof.
  • And an authentic-sounding job specification is not proof.

Sometimes the person on the other end is after your money. Sometimes they want your personal identity documents. Sometimes they want your corporate cloud credentials.

And as campaigns like Operation Dream Job prove, sometimes you aren't even the final target. The employer standing behind you is far more valuable.

So when a recruiter appears in your inbox, keep one question in mind:

A LinkedIn message can start the conversation. Verification should decide what happens next.
Technical Investigation Deep Dive

Interested in the Technical Side?

The non-technical guide ends here. Everything below walks through the cybersecurity and incident response architecture: how a Security Operations Center (SOC) investigates what happens after someone executes that malicious “technical assessment.”

Targeted at SOC analysts, threat hunters, detection engineers, and curious technologists.

Now Look at It From a SOC

Imagine an employee downloads:

C:\Users\victim\Downloads\Technical_Assessment.zip

They decompress the archive and double-click the file inside.

A few minutes later, an endpoint detection alert lands in the SOC triage queue.

Now the SOC analyst has to work backwards:

  • Where did the file originate? (Browser download, webmail, Slack, or messaging client?)
  • What process extracted the archive?
  • What binary or script executed immediately afterwards?
  • Did the file launch PowerShell, cmd.exe, cscript.exe, or another living-off-the-land binary (LOLBin)?
  • Did the endpoint establish outbound communication with an unfamiliar external IP address or newly registered domain?
  • Were secondary payloads staged or dropped into %AppData% or %TEMP%?
  • Were scheduled tasks, registry Run keys, or background services created to maintain persistence?
  • Did user authentication behavior suddenly change? Were unusual OAuth applications consented to?

Step by step, the investigation connects events that initially looked unrelated:

LinkedIn Message → Recruiter Conversation → Technical Assessment → File Execution → C2 Beaconing → Enterprise Compromise

What looked like a private, harmless job discussion to the employee becomes a critical incident timeline in the security incident report.

Why “Don't Open Suspicious Files” Isn't Enough

Traditional security awareness programs constantly remind employees:

“Don’t open suspicious attachments or click unexpected links.”

That is helpful advice in basic scenarios. But in recruitment attacks, there is an inherent failure mode:

The attachment was specifically engineered NOT to feel suspicious.

The threat actor invested days establishing rapport. You’ve reviewed job descriptions. You’ve discussed compensation. You are expecting the assessment.

Opening Technical_Assessment.zip feels like the obvious, professional next step.

That is why defending against social engineering cannot rely exclusively on individual human vigilance. Modern enterprise defense requires a multi-layered model encompassing continuous user education, email filtering, phishing-resistant FIDO2 authentication, endpoint detection and response (EDR), SIEM anomaly detection, and rapid incident response protocols [8] [10].

The human is one defensive layer. They must never be the only defensive layer.

What Would the SOC Actually Investigate?

When an endpoint alert fires, the analyst constructs an evidence timeline across five primary vectors:

1. Initial Delivery & Origin

Examine browser history, file download metadata (Mark-of-the-Web / Zone.Identifier), hosting domain reputation, and SSL certificate age for the staging server.

2. Execution & Process Trees

Trace parent-child process anomalies: did a PDF or Word document spawn powershell.exe or rundll32.exe? Were encoded commands or script bypass flags passed?

3. Network Telemetry & C2

Correlate endpoint socket connections with firewall and proxy logs. Check for outbound beaconing over TLS to dynamic DNS providers or unfamiliar foreign subnets.

4. Persistence & Identity

Inspect scheduled tasks, registry Run keys, startup folders, and WMI consumers. Check Entra ID / Okta logs for anomalous token requests or multi-factor fatigue attempts.

The LinkedIn Message Becomes Evidence

Understanding the human story before the alert is just as vital as analyzing the malware bytecode afterwards.

The security sensor tells the analyst what the machine did.

The recruiter conversation explains why the user did it.

Episode 07 — The Takeaway
  • Personalisation is not verification. Detailed knowledge of your projects, certifications, and tech stack only proves your profile was researched, not that the recruiter is real.
  • The conversation is the delivery vehicle. Attackers invest time building genuine rapport across LinkedIn, WhatsApp, and phone calls so that malicious files feel expected.
  • Notice when the objective shifts. Stop immediately if a recruitment interaction demands corporate SSO logins, executing unvetted code/archives, purchasing equipment, or providing bank details before an offer.
  • Always verify out-of-band. Never ask the recruiter to prove their own legitimacy. Cross-check open requisitions directly on the company’s official public careers portal.
  • In the SOC, the human context explains the machine alert. Understanding the LinkedIn lure helps analysts explain why an employee opened an assessment and where the compromise began.
