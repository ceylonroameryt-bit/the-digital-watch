Your Supplier Has Been Breached: A Playbook for When It Is Not Your Network

PS
Poorna Sujampathi Rathnayaka

The email comes from a supplier: "We are writing to inform you of a security incident." Your own systems show nothing wrong. But your staff's payroll records, your customers' details or a live connection into your network may be sitting with the company that was hit.

Modern organisations share data and access with dozens of suppliers: payroll and HR providers, IT support companies, software vendors, marketing platforms, cloud services. When one of them is breached, the incident becomes yours too, even though you did nothing wrong and cannot see inside their systems.

In June 2023, the UK payroll provider Zellis disclosed that its installation of the MOVEit file transfer software had been exploited. Staff data from several of its customers was stolen, with British Airways, the BBC and Boots among those affected. None of those organisations had been breached directly. CISA later reported that a criminal group known as Cl0p had begun exploiting a previously unknown flaw in MOVEit on 27 May 2023, stealing data from many organisations around the world.

You can outsource the work. You cannot outsource the responsibility for the data.
At a glance 1. Verify the notice is genuine
2. Get the facts from the supplier
3. Map your exposure: data, access and integrations
4. Contain: rotate shared secrets and limit connections
5. Decide on legal and regulatory reporting
6. Protect the people affected
7. Review the supplier relationship

1. Verify the Notice Is Real

Criminals send fake breach notices to trick people into clicking links or calling numbers. Contact the supplier through your existing account manager or the contact details in your contract, not the details in the message. Check the supplier's website or status page for a public statement.

2. Get the Facts

Suppliers often share very little at first. Ask these questions in writing, and record the answers and the time you received them:

  • What happened, and when? When did they detect it?
  • Which systems were affected, and is the attacker contained?
  • Was any of our data accessed or taken? Which records and which fields?
  • Were any credentials, API keys or connections to our systems exposed?
  • Do they have indicators of compromise, such as IP addresses or file names, that we should search for?
  • Have they reported to regulators or law enforcement? Who is investigating?
  • When will we get the next update, and who is our point of contact?

3. Map Your Own Exposure

Do not wait for the supplier's full investigation. Work out your own exposure in parallel:

  • Data. What personal or confidential data have you sent them, and how recently? Your data processing records and contracts should say.
  • Access into your network. Do they have VPN accounts, remote support tools, admin accounts or service accounts in your environment?
  • Integrations. Do they hold API keys, OAuth tokens, SFTP credentials or single sign-on connections to your systems?
  • Shared software. Do you run the same product that was exploited? In the MOVEit case, many organisations needed to patch their own installations as well as assess their suppliers.

4. Contain Your Side

  • Rotate anything shared. Change passwords, API keys, tokens and certificates used by or with the supplier.
  • Limit or suspend their access into your network until they confirm the incident is contained. Disable dormant supplier accounts entirely.
  • Hunt for the indicators they provide in your own logs, especially around any connections from their systems.
  • Watch supplier-related activity closely: sign-ins from supplier accounts, unusual data transfers and new integrations.
  • Patch your own copy of any affected software, if you use it.

5. Decide on Reporting

Under UK data protection law, if your supplier processes personal data on your behalf, you are usually the controller, and the duty to assess and report the breach is yours. A reportable personal data breach must be reported to the ICO within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, and the people affected must be told without undue delay if it is likely to result in a high risk to them.

The 72 hours run from when you become aware, which may be when the supplier tells you. Do not wait for their final report to start your assessment. Record what you knew, when, and why you made each decision. Check your contracts, insurance and any sector regulators too.

6. Protect the People Affected

  • Tell them clearly and early what happened, what data was involved and what you are doing. Vague statements damage trust more than the facts.
  • Warn about follow-up scams. Stolen payroll and customer data is often used for convincing phishing. Our guide Your Data Was in a Breach is a good basis for advice to staff.
  • Offer practical help where the data was sensitive, such as identity protection services or a point of contact for questions.
  • Brief your finance and HR teams to verify any bank detail changes by phone, as stolen payroll data can feed payroll diversion fraud.

7. Review the Supplier Relationship

Once the immediate incident is under control, look at what it revealed. The UK National Cyber Security Centre's supply chain guidance sets out twelve principles in four stages: understand the risks, establish control, check your arrangements and keep improving. Useful questions:

  • Did we know this supplier held that data or that access?
  • Did our contract require them to notify us promptly, and did they?
  • Did they need all the data we gave them? Could we share less?
  • Should their access be limited to specific times, systems or approvals?
  • Do we have the same exposure with other suppliers?

Final Thoughts

Supplier breaches are frustrating because so much is out of your hands. But a surprising amount is within your control: knowing what you shared, cutting off access quickly, meeting your own legal duties and looking after the people affected. The best time to answer "what does this supplier hold of ours?" is before they call.

Keep a list of what every supplier holds and what access they have. It turns a crisis into a checklist.
