Your Password Wasn't Hacked. Your Session Was Stolen.

Imagine this.

You log into your email in the morning.

Your password works.

Your phone asks you to approve the login.

You approve it.

Everything looks normal.

A few hours later, you discover someone else has been inside the account.

So naturally, the first thing you think is:

“Someone stole my password.”

You change it immediately.

But then something doesn't make sense.

There wasn't another MFA request.

You didn't approve another login.

And in some incidents, simply changing the password may not immediately deal with every already-established session.

So how did someone get in?

The answer can be surprisingly simple:

They may not have needed to log in again.

They may have stolen a session that had already been authenticated.

And once you understand what a session actually is, a lot of modern account-takeover attacks start making much more sense.

First, What Is a Session?

Think about what happens when you sign into Gmail, Microsoft 365, Facebook or another online service.

You enter your password.

Maybe you also use an authenticator app, security key, fingerprint or another MFA method.

The service checks everything and decides:

Yes, this is you.

But imagine if it asked you to repeat that entire process every time you opened another email or clicked another page.

Using the internet would become unbearable.

So after you successfully authenticate, the application creates a session.

OWASP describes a session identifier as the mechanism applications use to maintain an authenticated user's state while they continue interacting with the application. Cookies are one of the most common ways these session identifiers are exchanged between the browser and the website.

Authentication vs. session
  • Password → proves something you know
  • MFA → provides additional proof
  • Session → tells the service you've already been authenticated

Think of it like entering a festival.

At the entrance, someone checks your ticket and ID.

Once everything is verified, they put a wristband on you.

For the rest of the day, security doesn't check your ticket every time you move around.

They see the wristband.

You've already passed the entrance check.

A web session works on a similar principle.

And that's exactly why it can become valuable to an attacker.

What If Someone Copies the Wristband?

Imagine someone could make a perfect copy of your festival wristband.

They wouldn't need your ticket.

They wouldn't need your ID.

They wouldn't need to go through the same checks you went through.

They already have something the system recognises as belonging to someone who passed those checks.

That's essentially the problem behind session hijacking.

OWASP warns that disclosure or capture of a valid session identifier can allow an attacker to impersonate the legitimate user within the application.

Its cookie-theft guidance makes the consequence particularly clear: even as authentication becomes stronger through technologies such as 2FA and passkeys, a stolen valid session cookie can still potentially be used to hijack the session during its lifetime.

That's the important distinction.

The attacker isn't necessarily breaking your password.

They may be stealing something that exists after the password has already done its job.

So How Does a Session Get Stolen?

There isn't one single method.

Session theft can happen through malware, phishing, browser or application vulnerabilities, exposed session identifiers, compromised client-side code and other routes.

But three examples are particularly useful for understanding the problem.

1. Infostealer malware

You accidentally install malicious software.

Maybe it came through a fake download, cracked software, a malicious attachment or another infection route.

The malware runs on your device and looks for valuable browser information.

Session data can be one of those targets.

Google describes session theft as commonly occurring when malware running on a user's device extracts existing session cookies from the browser or waits for new sessions to be created and then exfiltrates those credentials to attacker-controlled infrastructure.

In that situation, the attacker doesn't necessarily need to convince you to type your password into a phishing page.

They're targeting what your browser already has.

2. Adversary-in-the-Middle phishing

This one is particularly interesting because everything can appear to work normally.

You receive an email:

A document has been shared with you.

Sign in with Microsoft 365 to view it.

You click.

A Microsoft login page appears.

You enter your email.

You enter your password.

Then Microsoft asks for MFA.

You approve it.

Nothing about that sequence immediately feels unusual.

Except you weren't communicating directly with Microsoft.

An attacker had placed phishing infrastructure between you and the legitimate service.

This technique is called Adversary-in-the-Middle, or AiTM, phishing.

Microsoft describes AiTM phishing as using a proxy server between the target and the website being impersonated. The proxy intercepts the authentication process and can extract information from the requests—including passwords and, critically, session cookies.

AiTM flow

YOU → Password + MFA → PHISHING PROXY → forwards authentication → REAL SERVICE → session created → PHISHING PROXY captures session → back to you.

You may genuinely have authenticated with the real service.

Your MFA may genuinely have been checked.

The problem is the attacker was sitting in the middle.

3. Client-side compromise

Session information can also be exposed because of vulnerabilities or malicious code running in the browser context.

This is where cookie configuration becomes important.

Websites have several controls available to reduce what can happen to cookies.

For example, OWASP recommends security attributes including:

HttpOnly — prevents ordinary browser scripts from directly reading the cookie through document.cookie.

Secure — tells the browser to send the cookie only over encrypted HTTPS connections.

SameSite — controls whether cookies are included with certain cross-site requests.

These are important protections, but each addresses particular attack scenarios rather than magically making a session impossible to compromise.

That's a recurring theme in browser security:

There usually isn't one setting that fixes everything.

“But I Have MFA”

This is where session theft becomes confusing.

You've probably been told:

Turn on MFA.

That's still good advice.

Suppose someone learns your Microsoft password.

They try to log in.

Microsoft asks them to approve the login through your second authentication factor.

They can't.

Access denied.

MFA has done exactly what it was designed to do.

But now consider the AiTM example.

You entered the password.

You completed MFA.

The legitimate service authenticated you.

Then the attacker obtained the resulting session.

Microsoft explains that once an attacker obtains a valid session cookie through this type of AiTM phishing, it can potentially be injected into another browser to skip the normal authentication process—even where MFA was enabled for the original login.

That's not the same as saying:

“MFA doesn't work.”

It does.

MFA protects authentication. A session exists after authentication. Those are related security problems, but they're not identical.

This Has Happened at Scale

This isn't just a theoretical attack from a cybersecurity textbook.

Microsoft investigated an AiTM phishing campaign that attempted to target more than 10,000 organisations beginning in September 2021.

Attackers spoofed Office 365 authentication pages and used AiTM infrastructure to intercept credentials and session cookies.

Microsoft subsequently observed attackers accessing affected mailboxes and conducting follow-on Business Email Compromise activity, including payment fraud.

Observed attack chain

Not simply: Password stolen → Account accessed

Closer to: PHISHING EMAIL → FAKE / PROXIED LOGIN → PASSWORD ENTERED → MFA COMPLETED → SESSION CAPTURED → SESSION REUSED → MAILBOX ACCESSED → FOLLOW-ON FRAUD

And this explains something victims can otherwise find difficult to understand:

“How did they get past my MFA?”

Sometimes they didn't independently pass it.

The legitimate user already completed the authentication process.

The attacker targeted what came afterwards.

Your Session Cookie Is Basically a Temporary Credential

This is probably the easiest way to think about it.

People understand that a password is sensitive.

We don't normally post passwords online.

We don't email them to strangers.

We know they need protection.

But session identifiers deserve similar care.

OWASP's guidance essentially treats a stolen valid session cookie as highly consequential because it can allow session hijacking for as long as that session remains usable.

A session cookie can look meaningless:
SESSION=eyJhbGciOi...

The value doesn't need to make sense to you. If the service recognises it as a valid authenticated session, that is what matters.

Why Cookie Settings Matter

Now we can go slightly deeper.

A cookie isn't simply a value.

Websites can attach rules controlling when and how browsers use it.

HttpOnly

A cookie marked HttpOnly isn't accessible to ordinary JavaScript through the browser's document.cookie interface.

That makes certain forms of cookie theft through Cross-Site Scripting harder.

But HttpOnly isn't a universal session-theft defence.

OWASP notes that while it protects the confidentiality of the cookie against direct script access, the browser may still include the cookie when sending legitimate requests.

Secure

The Secure attribute tells the browser to send the cookie only over HTTPS.

That helps prevent the session identifier from being exposed over an unencrypted HTTP connection.

SameSite

SameSite controls whether the browser includes a cookie with cross-site requests.

This is particularly useful as part of the defence against Cross-Site Request Forgery.

OWASP recommends treating it as defence in depth, rather than as a replacement for proper CSRF protections.

Different cookie controls, different problems
  • HttpOnly → limits script access
  • Secure → requires HTTPS transmission
  • SameSite → restricts cross-site cookie behaviour

Same objective: making the session harder to abuse.

Then There's Cookie Scope

This is where things become more subtle.

Cookies can also have Domain and Path attributes.

Those determine where they're valid.

Example cookie scope
example.com   login.example.com   shop.example.com   dev.example.com

If cookie scope is configured too broadly, a vulnerability in one part of the environment can potentially affect another.

OWASP specifically warns that overly permissive Domain settings can expose applications to cross-subdomain cookie attacks and recommends keeping cookie scope as narrow as practical.

Modern standards also provide cookie-name prefixes.

  • __Secure- requires the cookie to use the Secure attribute.
  • __Host- cookies must be Secure, cannot specify a Domain, and must use Path=/.

OWASP recommends __Host- for session identifiers because these restrictions help prevent certain forms of subdomain forgery and scope confusion.

This is where ideas such as session fixation and cookie injection become relevant.

But from a normal user's perspective, there's a simpler takeaway:

Session security isn't only about hiding the cookie.

How the application creates, scopes, rotates, expires and validates that session matters too.

Why Changing Your Password May Not Be the Whole Recovery

Now imagine you've fallen for an AiTM phishing page.

You realise what happened.

You immediately change your password.

Good.

You absolutely should.

But don't automatically assume the incident ends there.

If an attacker already obtained a valid session, the important question becomes whether that session has also been terminated.

OWASP recommends terminating sessions when authentication credentials change specifically to help stop ongoing abuse of already-stolen sessions.

After serious credential phishing, think beyond the password

CHANGE PASSWORD + REVOKE ACTIVE SESSIONS + CHECK DEVICES + CHECK ACCOUNT CHANGES

  • Look at recent sign-ins.
  • Look at devices.
  • Use Sign out everywhere or the equivalent if available.
  • Review connected applications.
  • For email, check forwarding settings and inbox rules.
  • For work accounts, report the incident to IT or your security team.

There's Another Problem: How Long Does a Session Live?

Sessions shouldn't last forever.

The longer a stolen session remains valid, the longer an attacker potentially has to use it.

OWASP therefore recommends both idle and absolute session timeouts, with timeout lengths chosen according to the sensitivity of the application.

Its guidance notes that insufficient session expiration increases exposure to session hijacking because an attacker can continue reusing a valid identifier while it remains active.

This creates a constant security-versus-usability problem.

Users hate being logged out every five minutes.

Attackers love sessions that remain valid for months.

Security teams have to find the balance.

Can We Detect a Stolen Session?

Sometimes.

Imagine you establish a session in London.

A normal session might look like:

Windows · Chrome · UK IP address · English language · Known device

Suddenly the same session appears with very different characteristics.

That doesn't automatically prove an attack.

People use VPNs.

They travel.

Networks change.

Browsers update.

But large changes can be useful risk signals.

OWASP recommends considering characteristics such as IP address, user agent and other client properties when looking for evidence that a session may have been hijacked. If significant anomalies appear, the service can require reauthentication or terminate the suspicious session.

That's why modern identity security increasingly looks beyond:

Is this password correct?

It also asks:

Does this session still behave like the person who created it?

What About Passkeys?

Passkeys are an important improvement.

They provide stronger phishing resistance than conventional passwords because users aren't typing a reusable secret into a website.

Google describes passkeys as phishing-resistant credentials tied to a user's device and protected using mechanisms such as a PIN or biometrics.

But there's an important distinction.

Passkeys strengthen the authentication process.

Session theft concerns what happens after successful authentication.

OWASP explicitly notes that even with stronger authentication such as 2FA and passkeys, a stolen valid session cookie can still potentially allow session hijacking while the session remains valid.

Which leads to a newer idea.

What If the Session Couldn't Simply Be Copied?

This is where Device Bound Session Credentials, or DBSC, become interesting.

The idea behind device-bound sessions

Traditional: Do you have the session?

Device-bound: Do you have the session + can you prove you are on the device that established it?

The private key isn't supposed to simply travel with the copied session credential.

So stealing session material and replaying it somewhere else becomes substantially harder.

Google has been deploying DBSC for Google Workspace, describing it as a way to strengthen protection after sign-in by binding a session more closely to the device where authentication occurred.

And the technology has continued moving beyond the research stage. In April 2026, Google said DBSC was entering public availability for Windows users in Chrome 146, with macOS expansion planned.

This is significant because it changes the basic assumption behind session security.

Instead of trusting a copied token simply because the token is valid, the service can demand additional cryptographic proof tied to the original device.

Does DBSC Solve Session Theft?

Not completely.

And it's important not to oversell it.

The W3C specification itself describes DBSC as an evolving web standard, and deployment depends on browser, operating-system and service support.

Attackers also don't stop adapting because one technique becomes harder.

Session security remains layered
  • That's why session security remains layered
  • Strong authentication
  • Phishing-resistant MFA
  • Passkeys
  • Secure cookie configuration
  • Shorter session lifetimes where appropriate
  • Risk-based reauthentication
  • Device intelligence
  • Malware protection
  • Session revocation
  • And increasingly, device-bound credentials

No single one replaces all the others.

So What Should You Actually Do?

For most people, you don't need to become an expert in cookies.

Practical steps for users
  • Use MFA
  • Prefer phishing-resistant authentication such as passkeys or hardware security keys where available
  • Keep your browser and operating system updated
  • Be selective about browser extensions
  • Avoid installing unknown software, especially cracked or unofficial downloads
  • If you think you've entered credentials into a phishing page, change the password through the legitimate service
  • But don't stop there
  • Check active sessions
  • Check devices
  • Check connected applications
  • Sign out of sessions you don't recognise
  • And if you suspect malware, treat the device itself as part of the incident

Because sometimes changing your password solves only half the problem.

The Login Screen Isn't the End of Security

A more realistic account-security chain

USERNAME + PASSWORD + MFA = SAFE is useful, but incomplete.

PASSWORD → MFA / PASSKEY → AUTHENTICATION → SESSION CREATED → SESSION MAINTAINED → ACCOUNT ACCESS

Every stage matters.

A strong password protects one stage.

MFA protects another.

A passkey can make phishing the authentication process significantly harder.

Secure session management protects what happens after you've successfully logged in.

And that's the part we don't talk about nearly enough.

Because sometimes an attacker doesn't need to know your password.

They don't need to guess your MFA code.

They don't even need to perform another login.

They just need something that tells the website you've already done all of that.

So the next time you hear about an account being compromised even though MFA was enabled, don't immediately assume the MFA was cracked.

Was the password actually stolen — or was the session stolen instead?

That difference matters.
