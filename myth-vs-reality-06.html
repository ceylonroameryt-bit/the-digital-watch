Myth: "A VPN Makes Me Secure"

"Hackers can't touch you." "Browse completely anonymously." "Military-grade encryption." VPN adverts are everywhere, and they have convinced a lot of people that a VPN is a security shield for everything they do online. It is not. It is a tunnel, and it matters a great deal who is at the other end.

The myth "I use a VPN, so I'm protected from hackers, malware and tracking, and nobody can see what I do online."

What a VPN Actually Does

A virtual private network creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a server run by the VPN provider. Your traffic travels through that tunnel, and websites see the VPN server's address instead of yours.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation's Surveillance Self-Defense guide describes VPNs as best suited for one thing: routing your network connection through a different network. That is genuinely useful when:

  • You do not trust the local network, such as hotel or café Wi-Fi, so people on that network cannot see which sites you visit.
  • You want to hide your browsing from your internet provider.
  • You need to reach your organisation's internal systems from outside the office, which is what VPNs were originally built for.
  • You need to get around network censorship or blocking.

Note that most websites already encrypt your connection with HTTPS, so even without a VPN, people on public Wi-Fi generally cannot read what you send. We looked at what public Wi-Fi really exposes in Episode 04.

What a VPN Does Not Do

  • It does not stop phishing. A fake login page works just as well through a VPN. You still type your password into it.
  • It does not stop malware. If you download something malicious, the VPN delivers it to you securely.
  • It does not make you anonymous. The EFF says plainly that a VPN is not a tool for anonymity. Websites can still recognise you through cookies, tracking pixels, browser fingerprinting and, most obviously, any account you sign in to.
  • It does not protect your accounts. Reused passwords, leaked credentials and missing multi-factor authentication are untouched by a VPN.
  • It does not fix an insecure device. Out-of-date software and weak settings remain exactly as they were.
A VPN does not remove the need to trust someone. It changes who you are trusting.

The Part the Adverts Leave Out: You Are Trusting the Provider

When you use a VPN, your internet provider can no longer see your browsing, but the VPN provider can. The EFF points out that while a VPN hides your browsing data from your internet provider, it is all visible to the VPN provider, and that many VPN providers save as much information as internet providers do. They are also subject to law enforcement requests.

Some VPN apps are worse than having none at all. A study led by Australia's national science agency, CSIRO, analysed 283 Android apps on Google Play that use the VPN permission. It found that 18% used tunnelling protocols without encryption, 75% included third-party tracking libraries, 82% requested access to sensitive data such as contacts and text messages, and four apps intercepted encrypted traffic by tampering with the device's certificate store. The study dates from 2016 and the app market has changed, but the lesson has not: a VPN app has a privileged view of all your traffic, so a bad one does real harm.

The EFF also warns that VPNs can be entirely free but sell your data, and that appearing in an official app store does not make a VPN app safe.

How to Use a VPN Sensibly

  • Decide whether you need one. For most people at home, HTTPS already protects the content of what they do. A VPN is most useful on untrusted networks, for work access, or where the internet is censored.
  • If your employer provides one, use it for work, as instructed.
  • Be wary of free VPNs. Ask how the service makes money. If you are not paying, your data may be the product.
  • Check the provider's privacy policy and track record, and look for independent audits, while remembering that a claim is not a guarantee.
  • Keep doing the basics, which matter far more: unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, updates, and caution with links and downloads.
  • For genuine anonymity, tools designed for it, such as the Tor Browser, are a better fit than a commercial VPN.
Verdict: Myth A VPN protects your traffic on the way to the VPN server and hides your browsing from your local network and internet provider. It does not stop phishing, malware or tracking, does not make you anonymous, and shifts your trust to the VPN company. Useful in the right situations; not a security suit.

Final Thoughts

VPNs are sold as a one-click answer to online security, and that is exactly the problem. People who believe it switch on the VPN and stop thinking about the things that actually cause harm. Use one when it solves a real problem, choose the provider carefully, and keep the rest of your defences in place.

A VPN is a tunnel, not a bodyguard. What you do at either end still matters.
