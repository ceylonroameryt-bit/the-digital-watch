The Box Under Your TV: How to Secure Your Home Router in Ten Minutes

Every message, video call, bank login and smart doorbell clip in your home passes through one small box. Most of us plugged it in years ago and have not thought about it since. Criminals have.

Think about the router in your home. It probably sits under the TV or in a hallway cupboard, blinking quietly. It came from your internet provider or from a shop, someone connected it on the day you moved in, and as long as the Wi-Fi works, nobody touches it.

That is exactly what makes it valuable to an attacker. A router that has not been updated for years, with settings nobody has checked, is an always-on computer with a direct connection to the internet and no one watching it.

The good news is that securing it is one of the quickest wins in home security. You do not need any technical background. You need the password on the sticker, a phone or laptop, and about ten minutes.

Your router is the front door of your digital home. Most people have never checked whether it locks.

1. What Your Router Actually Does

Your router does two jobs. It connects your home to the internet, and it runs the Wi-Fi network that your phones, laptops, TV, speakers and cameras join.

Because everything goes through it, a compromised router can quietly do a lot of damage. Depending on what an attacker manages to change, it can be used to watch which websites you visit, send you to fake versions of real sites, attack other devices on your network, or hide the attacker's own activity behind your internet connection.

That last one is more common than most people realise, and it is why your router matters even if you think you have nothing worth stealing.

2. Why Criminals Want Old Routers

In January 2024, the US Justice Department announced that the FBI had disrupted a network of hijacked routers in a court-authorised operation the previous month. The network, known as the KV Botnet, was used by a state-sponsored hacking group known as Volt Typhoon to hide its activity while targeting critical infrastructure.

The routers in that network did not belong to governments or large companies. According to the Justice Department, the vast majority were small office and home routers made by Cisco and NetGear that had reached "end of life", meaning the manufacturers no longer released security updates for them.

In May 2025 the FBI issued a public warning about a similar problem. Criminals were infecting end-of-life routers with a new version of malware called TheMoon and then selling access to them as "proxies". Anyone paying for the service could route their traffic through someone's home router, so websites would record the homeowner's internet address rather than the criminal's.

Why this matters to you If your router is used as a proxy, the trail of someone else's crime can lead back to your internet connection. The FBI said routers from 2010 or earlier were most at risk, and that the malware targeted devices with remote administration switched on.

Both cases share the same two ingredients: old routers that no longer receive updates, and settings that let the router be reached from the internet. Those are exactly the two things you can fix tonight.

3. The Ten-Minute Checklist

Step 1: Open your router's settings. Look at the sticker on the router. Most show a web address (often something like 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1), or the name of an app made by the manufacturer or your internet provider. Connect to your Wi-Fi, open that address or app, and sign in with the admin password on the sticker.

Step 2: Change the admin password. This is the password that controls the router itself, not the Wi-Fi password. If it is still the one on the sticker, anyone who has seen the sticker can change your settings. Replace it with a long password that you do not use anywhere else, and save it in your password manager. CISA, the US cyber security agency, lists this as one of the first things to do.

Step 3: Update the firmware. Firmware is the router's own software. Look for "Firmware update", "Software update" or "System" in the settings and install anything available. If there is an option for automatic updates, turn it on.

Step 4: Turn off remote management. Sometimes called "remote access" or "web access from WAN", this lets the router's settings be reached from the internet. Very few households need it. Both the FBI and CISA recommend switching it off.

Step 5: Turn off WPS. Wi-Fi Protected Setup lets devices join by pressing a button or entering a PIN. CISA warns that a design flaw in the PIN method makes it much easier for an attacker to guess. You can still join devices the normal way with your Wi-Fi password.

Step 6: Use the strongest Wi-Fi encryption offered. Choose WPA3 if your router and devices support it. If not, use WPA2 with AES. Avoid anything labelled WEP, WPA (without a number) or TKIP.

Step 7: Switch off UPnP unless something needs it. Universal Plug and Play lets devices on your network open connections to the internet by themselves. CISA describes it as a convenience that is also a security risk. If a games console or app stops working afterwards, you can turn it back on.

Step 8: Give visitors and gadgets a guest network. Many routers can run a second Wi-Fi network. Put visitors, and smart devices such as plugs, bulbs and speakers, on the guest network, so that if one of them is compromised it is kept apart from your laptops and phones.

You do not need to understand every setting. Change the password, update it, and close the doors you are not using.

4. Is Your Router Too Old?

No amount of careful configuration can fix a router that no longer receives security updates. If a flaw is discovered, it will never be patched.

To check, find the model number on the sticker and search for it on the manufacturer's website together with "end of life" or "support". If the latest firmware is several years old, or the model is listed as discontinued with no further updates, the FBI's advice is straightforward: replace it with a supported model.

Two practical notes:

  • If your router came from your internet provider, the provider often updates it automatically. Ask them whether your model is still supported, and whether they will send a newer one. Many will do so free of charge.
  • If you are buying a new router in the UK, the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure rules, in force since 29 April 2024, require manufacturers to avoid universal default passwords and to publish how long they will provide security updates. Check that period before you buy.

5. Signs Something Is Wrong

The FBI listed a few signs that a router may have been compromised:

  • The router runs unusually hot.
  • Your connection keeps dropping or slowing down for no obvious reason.
  • Settings have changed that you do not recognise, such as a new admin account, different DNS servers or remote management turned back on.

If you notice these, update the firmware, change the admin password and restart the router. If the router is out of support, replace it. A factory reset (usually a small button held down for several seconds) followed by setting it up again with the checklist above is also a reasonable step.

Final Thoughts

Most of us lock the front door without thinking about it. The router is the same idea for your digital life, and it only needs attention a few times a year.

Set a reminder for the first weekend of every few months to sign in, check for updates and make sure remote management is still off. If your router is more than a few years old and no longer receives updates, treat replacing it as a small, worthwhile cost rather than an optional upgrade.

An updated router with a strong password and remote access switched off is no longer an easy target. That is all it takes.
