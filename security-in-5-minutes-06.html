Lock Down Your Google or Microsoft Account: A Fifteen-Minute Check-Up

For most people, one account sits above all the others. It holds your email, your photos, your files and often your saved passwords. If someone takes it over, they can reset almost everything else. That makes it the account most worth fifteen minutes of your time.

If you use an Android phone, Gmail or Chrome, that account is probably your Google Account. If you use Windows, Outlook, Xbox or OneDrive, it is probably your Microsoft account. Many people have both.

These accounts are under constant attack. In May 2025, Microsoft said it had seen around 7,000 password attacks every second over the previous year, more than double the rate of 2023. Almost all of them are automated: tools trying leaked passwords, guessing common ones, or sending convincing sign-in pages.

Both Google and Microsoft now give you excellent tools to shut those attacks out. Most of them are switched off or half set up on the average account. This check-up walks through them in order.

Whoever controls your main account controls the "forgot password" link for everything else.

1. Open the Right Page

Do this on a computer or phone you trust, and type the address yourself rather than following a link:

  • Google: go to myaccount.google.com and open the Security section. Google also offers a guided Security Checkup at myaccount.google.com/security-checkup.
  • Microsoft: go to account.microsoft.com and open Security.

Work through the steps below on each account you use.

2. Switch to a Passkey

Both companies have moved firmly towards passkeys, which we explained in Episode 01. A passkey cannot be typed into a fake sign-in page, because it only works on the real website.

Google made passkeys the default option for personal accounts in October 2023. If you have not created one yet, you will usually be prompted at your next sign-in, or you can add one under Security. Google turned on a setting called "Skip password when possible" at the same time, so your account prefers the passkey whenever you have one. Google says passkeys are 40% faster than passwords.

Microsoft announced in May 2025 that new Microsoft accounts would be passwordless by default. If you have an older account, you can add a passkey, use Windows Hello, or use the Microsoft Authenticator app, and then remove your password entirely from your account settings. Microsoft reported that people signing in with passkeys succeeded about 98% of the time, compared with 32% for passwords.

Add more than one Create passkeys on at least two devices, such as your phone and your laptop, or add a hardware security key. Then a single lost or broken device does not lock you out.

3. Fix Your Recovery Details

Recovery details are how you get back in if something goes wrong, and they are also how an attacker might try to get in. Google lists unexpected changes to your recovery phone or email as one of the clearest signs of a hacked account.

  • Recovery phone: make sure it is your current number. Remove old numbers you no longer use.
  • Recovery email: make sure it is an address you still check and that is itself protected. Remove old work or university addresses.
  • Recovery codes: Microsoft offers a recovery code, and Google offers backup codes for two-step verification. Generate them, print them or save them in your password manager, and keep them somewhere safe.

4. Check Which Devices Are Signed In

Both accounts show every phone, tablet and computer currently signed in.

  • Google: under Security, find Your devices and select Manage all devices.
  • Microsoft: go to the Devices section of your account.

Sign out of anything you do not recognise, and anything you no longer own: old phones, a laptop you sold, a friend's computer you once used.

5. Review Recent Security Activity

Google shows Recent security events, and Microsoft shows sign-in activity. Look for sign-ins from places you have not been, or changes you did not make. If you see something, Google's advice is to select "No, it wasn't me" and follow the prompts, which will take you through securing the account.

6. Remove Apps You No Longer Use

Over the years, you have probably used "Sign in with Google" or "Sign in with Microsoft" for dozens of apps and websites. Each one may still have some access to your account.

Google lists these under "Your connections to third-party apps and services", and Microsoft under "Apps and services" in your privacy or account settings. Remove anything you do not recognise or no longer use. Google notes that removing access stops the app reaching your data in future, although you may need to ask the developer to delete data it already holds.

7. Check Your Email for Hidden Rules

This step is easy to forget and is one of the first things attackers set up. A forwarding rule quietly sends copies of your email to someone else. A filter can hide security alerts or replies from your bank.

  • Gmail: Settings, then See all settings, then check Forwarding and POP/IMAP and Filters and blocked addresses.
  • Outlook.com: Settings, then Mail, then check Forwarding and Rules.

Delete any forwarding address or rule you did not create yourself.

8. If You Are at Higher Risk

Journalists, campaigners, politicians, people going through a difficult separation and anyone who may be personally targeted should go further. Google offers the Advanced Protection Program, which requires passkeys or security keys and adds stricter checks on account recovery and downloads. For Microsoft accounts, removing the password entirely and relying on passkeys and a hardware security key gives a similar level of protection.

Final Thoughts

None of these steps is difficult on its own. Together they close almost every route an attacker would use: guessed or leaked passwords, phished codes, forgotten devices, old recovery details and hidden email rules.

Put a reminder in your calendar to repeat this check-up twice a year. It takes less time each time you do it.

A passkey, current recovery details and a quick look at devices and email rules. Fifteen minutes, twice a year.
