Black Friday Scams: A Ten-Minute Checklist Before You Shop

Fake shops, fake delivery messages and "unbeatable" deals all get busier when people are hunting for bargains. A few habits, applied in about ten minutes, close most of the gaps.

Black Friday falls on 27 November this year, with Cyber Monday on 30 November. Scammers know the calendar as well as retailers do. They count on you moving quickly, comparing prices across many tabs and clicking whatever looks like a deal.

Nothing here needs special software. It is a set of questions to ask before you hand over a card number or tap a link.

Urgency is the scammer's main tool. Anything that rushes you is a reason to slow down.

1. Fake Shops That Look Real

A fake shop copies the look of a genuine retailer, or invents a new brand, and advertises well-known products at prices that seem generous. The customer pays and receives a counterfeit item or nothing at all. Advertisements on social media and search results are a common route to these sites, so a polished advert is not evidence of a real business.

Before buying from a shop you do not know:

  • Type the address yourself for brands you already use, rather than following an advert or a message.
  • Search the shop's name together with the word "scam" or "reviews" and read what independent sites say, not just the reviews on the shop's own pages.
  • Be wary of steep discounts on luxury or in-demand items. A price far below every other seller is a warning.
  • Look for a real address, a phone number and clear returns terms. Missing or vague details are a reason to walk away.
  • Do not trust the padlock on its own. It means the connection is encrypted, not that the owner is honest. Our padlock myth article explains why.

2. Delivery and Order Messages You Did Not Expect

During busy weeks you really are expecting parcels, which makes a fake "missed delivery" or "pay a small redelivery fee" text believable. The link leads to a page that collects card details or login information.

  • Do not tap links in unexpected parcel messages. Open the carrier's app or type its website address yourself and enter the tracking number there.
  • Check your orders in your account at the retailer, not through a message.
  • A genuine carrier will not need your bank login. If a page asks for it, close it.
  • If you did tap and enter details, treat it as a phishing incident: contact your card issuer and change any reused password. See our phishing link guide.

3. Pay in a Way You Can Dispute

How you pay matters most if something goes wrong. Consumer-protection agencies commonly warn that requests for payment by gift card, bank or wire transfer, payment app or cryptocurrency are a strong sign of a scam, because those payments are difficult to reverse.

  • Prefer a credit card, or a card with strong dispute rights. Protections differ between countries and card types, so check what your own provider offers.
  • Consider a single-use or virtual card number if your bank provides one.
  • Use a wallet such as Apple Pay or Google Pay where a shop supports it, so the shop never sees your real card number.
  • Never pay a stranger by bank transfer to "hold" a deal, however convincing the story.

4. Keep Your Shopping Accounts Hard to Take Over

  • Use a unique password for each retailer, ideally from a password manager (see our password manager guide).
  • Turn on passkeys or two-step sign-in wherever the shop offers it. Our passkeys guide shows what to switch to first.
  • Check for stored cards you no longer need and remove them.
  • Prefer guest checkout for a shop you are only using once.

5. Spot the Pressure Tactics

  • Countdown timers and "only 2 left" banners on unfamiliar sites. Real shops use them too, so treat them as neutral, not as proof.
  • Emails or messages claiming your account is locked unless you act now.
  • Prize draws, surveys and "exclusive vouchers" that ask for card details.
  • Deals shared by friends on messaging apps: their account may have been hijacked.

6. If You Think You Have Been Scammed

  • Contact your bank or card provider straight away and ask about blocking the card and disputing the payment.
  • Change the password of any account that shares the one you entered, starting with email.
  • Keep screenshots of the advert, the website, the order confirmation and any messages.
  • Report it. In the United States use ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI's IC3. In the UK use Action Fraud. Reporting helps others and can help take the site down.

Final Thoughts: A Good Deal Can Wait Ten Minutes

Real sales do not vanish while you check a shop's reputation or open your parcel tracker in a new tab. If a deal only works when you do not stop to think, it is not a deal.

Check the shop, pay in a way you can dispute, and open the delivery app yourself.
