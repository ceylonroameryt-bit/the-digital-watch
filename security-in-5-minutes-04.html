Backups That Survive Ransomware: The 3-2-1-1-0 Rule Made Simple

Everyone agrees you should back up your files. Fewer people realise that ransomware is designed to find those backups and destroy them first. A backup only protects you if the attacker cannot reach it.

Imagine switching on your laptop and finding every photo, document and spreadsheet renamed with a strange extension. A note on the desktop explains that your files have been encrypted and gives a price for getting them back.

Your first thought is probably relief: you have a backup. Then you plug in the external drive, or open the cloud folder, and find the same strange file names there too.

This is not bad luck. Ransomware is built to look for backups, because a victim with a working backup has no reason to pay. In Sophos's 2024 survey of organisations hit by ransomware, 94% said the attackers had tried to compromise their backups during the attack, and 57% of those attempts succeeded.

The question is not "do I have a backup?" It is "could ransomware on my computer reach it?"

1. Why Ordinary Backups Fail

Most home backups fail in one of three ways.

The drive is always plugged in. An external drive that stays connected looks to ransomware like just another folder. It gets encrypted along with everything else. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) warns that a backup drive should not be left connected to your computer, for exactly this reason.

Cloud sync is mistaken for a backup. Services such as OneDrive, Google Drive, iCloud Drive and Dropbox keep a folder on your computer in step with the cloud. That is useful, but it means that when your files are encrypted, the encrypted versions are copied to the cloud too. CISA, the US cyber security agency, warns that automated cloud backups on their own may not be enough for this reason.

Nobody has ever tried restoring from it. Backups silently stop working all the time: a full drive, an expired subscription, a setting that changed after an update. You only find out when you need it.

2. The 3-2-1-1-0 Rule in Plain Language

The classic rule, which the NCSC describes, is 3-2-1: at least three copies of your data, on two different devices, with one kept somewhere else. Backup specialists have since added two more numbers to deal with ransomware.

3 copies of your data The original on your device, plus two backups.
2 different types of storage For example, an external drive and a cloud backup service, so one failure does not take out both.
1 copy away from home Usually the cloud, so a fire, flood or burglary does not destroy every copy.
1 copy that ransomware cannot change Either offline (a drive you unplug after each backup) or a cloud service that keeps older versions which cannot be overwritten straight away.
0 surprises when you restore You have actually tested getting files back, and it worked.

The last two numbers are the ones that matter most against ransomware. The NCSC's guidance on offline backups makes the same point in different words: keep several copies so that if one is compromised, another remains, and test them regularly to check they work.

3. A Simple Home Setup That Follows the Rule

You do not need special equipment. For most households, this is enough:

  1. Your files on your computer and phone. That is copy one.
  2. A cloud backup with version history. Use a proper backup service, or make sure your cloud storage keeps previous versions of files and a recycle bin you can restore from. Turn on version history if it is optional. That is copy two, and it lives away from home.
  3. An external drive you unplug. Use your computer's built-in backup tool (Time Machine on a Mac, File History or Windows Backup on Windows), plug the drive in once a week, let it run, then disconnect it and put it away. That is copy three, and it is offline most of the time.

For phones, the built-in iCloud or Google backup covers photos, messages and settings. Check it is switched on and that it has backed up recently. For irreplaceable photos, copy them to the external drive every few months as well.

Protect the backup account too CISA notes that attackers look for saved login details to reach backup systems. Your cloud backup account deserves the same protection as your email: a unique password and two-step verification, ideally a passkey (see Episode 01).

4. Test It in Five Minutes

A backup you have never restored from is a hope, not a plan. Once every few months:

  • Pick three random files: a recent document, an older photo and something from a folder you rarely open.
  • Restore them from the cloud backup to a different folder, and open them.
  • Restore the same files from the external drive and open them too.
  • Check the date of the latest backup on both. If either is weeks old, find out why.

If all six files open, your backups work. That is the "0" in 3-2-1-1-0.

5. If Ransomware Does Hit

Having good backups changes what happens next. In the same Sophos survey, 68% of organisations whose data was encrypted got it back using backups.

  • Disconnect the affected device from the internet and your home network.
  • Do not plug in your backup drive to the infected computer.
  • Get the device cleaned or reset before restoring anything. The NCSC advises checking backups for malware before restoring, because an infection may have been present for some time.
  • Report it. In the UK, report to Action Fraud. The NCSC points out that paying does not guarantee you will get your data back.

Final Thoughts

Ransomware works because it removes your options. A backup that the attacker cannot reach gives them back.

This week, check that your cloud storage keeps file versions, buy or dust off an external drive, run one backup and unplug it. Then put a reminder in your calendar to test a restore every few months.

One copy in the cloud with version history, one copy on a drive in a drawer, and a test now and then. That is ransomware-resistant backup for a home.
