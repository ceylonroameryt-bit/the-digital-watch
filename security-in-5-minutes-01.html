Passkeys, Text Codes or an Authenticator App: Which Should You Switch To First?

You already have two-step verification switched on. The code arrives, you type it in, and you feel safe. But the way that code reaches you, and the way you hand it over, decides how much it really protects you.

Picture someone who runs a small online shop. One evening a text arrives that looks exactly like the ones her payment provider sends: a sign-in attempt from a new device, and a link to "secure your account".

She tapped it, typed her password, and then typed the six-digit code that had just arrived on her phone. The page said thank you. Ten minutes later her payout details had changed.

She had done what everyone tells you to do. She had two-step verification turned on. The problem was not that she lacked a second step. The problem was that the second step was something she could be tricked into typing into the wrong website.

That is the real difference between the three options most services now offer: a code sent by text message, a code from an authenticator app, and a passkey. This article explains what each one protects you from, where each one falls short, and which accounts to upgrade first. Give it five minutes and you will know exactly what to change tonight.

Two-step verification is not one thing. A text code, an app code and a passkey stop very different attacks.

1. The Three Options, in Plain Language

A text message code (SMS). After you enter your password, the service sends a short code to your phone number. You type the code back in. It proves you have access to that phone number at that moment.

An authenticator app code. Apps such as Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator or the one built into many password managers show a six-digit code that changes every 30 seconds. The code is calculated on your phone from a secret that was set up once, usually by scanning a QR code. Nothing travels over the mobile network.

A passkey. Instead of a password and a code, your device holds a cryptographic key that belongs to one website or app. When you sign in, you unlock it with your fingerprint, face or phone PIN, and your device proves to the website that it holds the right key. There is nothing for you to read, copy or type.

The first two are things you type. The third is something your device does. Keep that distinction in mind, because it is the whole story.

2. Why Text Message Codes Are the Weakest Link

Text codes have three separate problems.

Your phone number can be stolen. In a SIM swap, a criminal persuades your mobile provider to move your number onto a SIM card they control, usually by pretending to be you. From that moment, your texts, including every sign-in code, go to them. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center received 1,611 SIM swapping complaints in 2021, with reported losses of more than $68 million. Across the three years before that, it had received 320.

Text messages are not encrypted. In December 2024, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) published mobile security guidance after investigating intrusions into telecoms networks. Its advice to people likely to be targeted was direct: do not use SMS as a second factor, and move to phishing-resistant methods instead.

You can be tricked into handing the code over. This is the one that caught the shop owner in our example. A fake login page asks for your password, passes it to the real site in the background, and then asks for the code the real site has just texted you. You type it in, the attacker uses it within seconds, and they are inside your account.

What the standards body says The latest US digital identity guidelines from NIST (SP 800-63B-4) class verification over the phone network as "restricted", meaning services that rely on it must manage the extra risk. The same document states that codes delivered this way, and one-time codes in general, are not phishing-resistant.

3. That Does Not Make Text Codes Useless

Before you panic and switch everything off, here is the other side.

In 2019, Google worked with researchers from New York University and the University of California, San Diego to measure how well different sign-in protections held up against real attacks. An SMS code sent to a recovery phone blocked 100% of automated bot attacks, 96% of bulk phishing attacks and 76% of targeted attacks.

Most account break-ins are not a skilled criminal focused on you personally. They are automated tools trying leaked passwords against thousands of accounts. Against those, even a text code is a very effective wall.

So the rule is simple: a text code is far better than no second step at all. It is just the wrong place to stop.

4. Authenticator Apps: Better, but Still Something You Type

An authenticator app fixes the first two problems with text messages. Your code is generated on your phone, so it cannot be intercepted on the mobile network, and it does not depend on your phone number, so a SIM swap does not hand it to someone else.

It does not fix the third problem. A code from an app is still six digits that you read and type, and a convincing fake page can ask for it just as easily as it asks for a text code.

The clearest real example comes from the internet company Cloudflare. In July 2022, attackers sent more than 100 text messages to Cloudflare employees and their families, pointing them to a convincing copy of the company's login page. At least 76 employees received the messages. Three of them entered their username and password.

The fake page also asked for a one-time code, and Cloudflare later explained that the attackers were relaying those codes to themselves in real time so they could use them before they expired. Against a company that relied on app codes, that would probably have been enough.

It failed at Cloudflare for one reason: every employee signed in with a hardware security key, which only works on the genuine website. The attackers had valid passwords and nothing to do with them. Twilio, which was hit by a similar campaign at around the same time, disclosed that customer data was accessed.

The question is not "do I have a second step?" It is "could a fake website collect my second step?"

5. Passkeys: The Step a Fake Website Cannot Steal

A passkey is built on the same technology as the security keys that protected Cloudflare, but it lives on the phone, tablet or computer you already own.

When you create a passkey, your device makes a matched pair of keys. The website keeps one half, which is useless on its own. Your device keeps the other half, locked behind your fingerprint, face or screen PIN. Crucially, the passkey is tied to the exact website address it was created for.

If a criminal sends you to a lookalike page, your device simply does not offer the passkey, because the address does not match. There is no code on screen for you to be talked into typing. NIST describes this as "verifier name binding", and it is the reason passkeys count as phishing-resistant while text and app codes do not.

Two other worries come up often:

  • Is my fingerprint or face sent to the website? No. According to the FIDO Alliance, the industry body behind passkeys, your biometric data stays on your device. The website only learns that the check succeeded.
  • What if I lose my phone? Most passkeys sync through your Apple, Google or password manager account, protected by end-to-end encryption, so they reappear when you sign in on a new device. Keep that main account well protected, and keep a backup sign-in method on your most important accounts.

In April 2026 the UK's National Cyber Security Centre changed its public advice. It now recommends passkeys wherever a service supports them, and two-step verification where it does not, and it describes traditional methods, including password plus SMS and app codes, as phishable.

6. The Order to Switch Your Accounts

You do not need to upgrade everything tonight. Start with the accounts that unlock other accounts.

  1. Your main email account. Almost every "forgot password" link lands here. Whoever controls your email can reset nearly everything else.
  2. Your Apple, Google or Microsoft account. This is often where your passkeys, photos, backups and saved passwords live.
  3. Your password manager, if you use one.
  4. Banking and payment apps. Many banks use their own app approval instead of passkeys. That is fine; follow your bank's own guidance.
  5. Social media and messaging, especially accounts that people trust to be you.
  6. Work accounts, following your employer's policy.

For each account, open the security settings and pick the strongest option offered, in this order:

First choice: a passkey Look for "Passkeys", "Sign in with a passkey" or "Security keys" in the security or sign-in settings.
Second choice: an authenticator app If there is no passkey option, set up app codes and, where the service allows it, remove text messages as a sign-in method.
Last resort: text message codes Keep them only where nothing else is offered. They still beat having no second step.

7. Your Five-Minute Checklist

  • Add a passkey to your main email account today, from the device you use most.
  • Add a second passkey or backup method on another device, so one lost phone does not lock you out.
  • Save your recovery codes somewhere offline or in your password manager.
  • Ask your mobile provider about a SIM swap PIN or account password, so your number cannot be moved with a phone call.
  • Remember one rule: a genuine service will never ask you to read a sign-in code out to a caller. Anyone who does is the attacker.

Final Thoughts

Go back to the shop owner at the start of this article. If she had signed in with a passkey, the fake page would have received her password and nothing else. There would have been no code on her screen to give away, and her phone would not have offered the passkey to a website it did not recognise.

That is the point of this upgrade. Text codes and app codes rely on you spotting the trick every single time. A passkey does not ask you to be perfect. It simply refuses to work anywhere except the real website.

Turn on whatever second step you can today. Then, one account at a time, move the important ones to passkeys.
