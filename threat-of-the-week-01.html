MFA Fatigue: How Attackers Spam Your Phone Until You Tap Approve

PS
Poorna Sujampathi Rathnayaka
Writer · Cyber Insight · Threat of the Week

💬 Feedback
T1621
MITRE ATT&CK technique

It is late at night and your phone buzzes: "Approve sign-in?" You did not try to sign in, so you tap Deny. It buzzes again. And again. Twenty times. Eventually someone gets tired, distracted or confused, and taps Approve. That is the whole attack.

Push notifications made two-step verification much easier to use. Instead of typing a code, you tap a button on your phone. That convenience created a new weakness: if an attacker already has your password, they can make your phone ask you to let them in, over and over, until you do.

This technique is known as MFA fatigue, MFA bombing or push bombing. It requires no malware and no technical skill beyond having the password. It works on people, not systems, and it has been used against some of the best-resourced technology companies in the world.

MFA fatigue does not break two-step verification. It persuades the user to switch it off for one sign-in.
MITRE ATT&CK mapping Technique: T1621, Multi-Factor Authentication Request Generation
Tactic: Credential Access (TA0006)
Usually follows: T1078 Valid Accounts, with the password obtained through phishing, infostealer malware or a data breach
Groups known to use it, according to MITRE: LAPSUS$, Scattered Spider and APT29

1. How the Attack Works

  1. The attacker gets the password. It may have been phished, stolen by malware on a personal device, bought from a criminal marketplace or reused from another breach.
  2. They try to sign in. The password is correct, so the service sends a push request to the real user's phone.
  3. The user denies it. The attacker simply tries again, sometimes dozens of times, often late at night or during the working day when people are busy.
  4. Pressure is added. Some attackers message or phone the victim pretending to be IT support: "We are fixing a problem with your account. Please accept the next prompt so it stops."
  5. The user approves one. By mistake, out of frustration, or because they believe the caller. The attacker is in.

2. A Real Case: Uber, September 2022

In September 2022, Uber disclosed a serious breach of its internal systems. In its own security update, the company explained what happened at the start.

Uber said the attacker had likely bought a contractor's corporate password on the dark web, after malware on the contractor's personal device exposed it. The attacker then repeatedly tried to log in. Each attempt sent a two-factor approval request to the contractor, which initially blocked access. Eventually, the contractor accepted one, and the attacker logged in.

From there, Uber said, the attacker accessed other employee accounts and gained elevated permissions to tools including G-Suite and Slack. Uber linked the attacker to the LAPSUS$ group and said it was strengthening its multi-factor authentication policies.

The lesson is not that the contractor was careless. Anyone can make a mistake after the fifteenth prompt. The lesson is that a security control which can be defeated by one tired tap is not strong enough on its own.

3. Why It Works

  • The prompt looks genuine because it is genuine. It comes from the real service, through the real app.
  • One-tap approval gives very little context. Many prompts show only an app name and a rough location, which users rarely read.
  • Repetition wears people down. Denying the same message again and again is annoying, and approving makes it stop.
  • A phone call adds authority. When someone claiming to be IT support explains the prompts, approving one feels like the helpful thing to do.

4. How to Stop It

Best: phishing-resistant MFA. CISA, the US cyber security agency, recommends phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys and FIDO2 security keys as the first choice. There is no approval button to spam. The user's device only responds to the genuine website, and only when the user is actually signing in. Its advisory on Scattered Spider notes that these methods are not susceptible to push bombing.

Good interim fix: number matching. Where phishing-resistant MFA is not yet possible, CISA recommends number matching. The sign-in screen shows a number, and the user must type that number into their authenticator app to approve. An attacker can send as many prompts as they like, but the user cannot approve them without seeing the attacker's screen. Microsoft began enforcing number matching for all Microsoft Authenticator push notifications on 8 May 2023.

Also worth doing:

  • Limit how many prompts can be sent in a short period, as MITRE recommends, and lock or flag the account when the limit is reached.
  • Show more context in prompts, such as the application and location, so users can spot a sign-in that is not theirs.
  • Use conditional access to block sign-ins from unexpected locations or devices that do not meet your policy.

5. How Defenders Can Detect It

MFA fatigue leaves a recognisable trail in identity logs. MITRE's detection guidance for T1621 and Microsoft's documentation point to the same signals:

  • A burst of MFA requests for one account within a short window, especially ones that are denied or time out.
  • A successful MFA approval immediately after several denials. Treat this as a likely compromise, not a user who finally remembered signing in.
  • Correct password, unusual origin. Sign-ins from an IP address, country or device that does not match where the user's MFA device is.
  • User-reported prompts. In Microsoft Entra ID, the "Report suspicious activity" feature lets users flag a prompt they did not start. Microsoft says this marks the user as high risk in Entra ID Protection and appears as a risk detection, so it can trigger an automatic response such as a password reset.
Response when you see it The repeated prompts prove the attacker has the password. Reset it, revoke the user's active sessions, review recent sign-ins and any MFA methods added to the account, and check for new mailbox rules or app consents. Then move that user to phishing-resistant MFA.

6. What Everyone Should Know

If you are not a defender, this is the part that matters:

  • Only approve a sign-in you started, seconds ago, yourself.
  • An unexpected prompt means someone has your password. Deny it, then change your password straight away.
  • Report it using the app's "report" option if it has one, or tell your IT team.
  • Never approve a prompt because someone asked you to by phone, text or chat, even if they say they are from IT support.
  • Switch to a passkey where you can, as described in Security in 5 Minutes, Episode 01.

Final Thoughts

MFA fatigue is a reminder that security controls are only as strong as the moment a tired person has to make a decision. The answer is not to tell people to try harder. It is to remove the decision: use sign-in methods that cannot be approved by accident, and treat a flood of prompts as the alarm it really is.

Twenty prompts in a row is not a glitch. It is an attacker with the password, knocking until someone answers.
