Deepfake Voice and Video Scams: When the Boss on the Call Is Not Real

A finance employee joins a video call with the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. They look right and sound right. The CFO explains a confidential deal and asks for transfers to be made. Everyone on the call, except the employee, is a fake.

For years, a phone call or a video call was the gold standard for checking whether an unusual request was genuine. If the email seemed odd, you would ring the person. If you could see their face, you could be sure.

Generative AI has weakened that assumption. A few seconds of someone's voice from a video or voicemail can be enough to produce a convincing clone, and live deepfake video is good enough to fool people who are not expecting it. In December 2024, the FBI warned that criminals were using generative AI across text, images, audio and video to make fraud more convincing.

We introduced deepfakes for a general audience in Can You Still Trust What You See and Hear Online? This episode looks at how they are used against organisations, and how to defend against them.

You will not reliably spot a good deepfake. The defence is a process that does not depend on spotting one.
MITRE ATT&CK mapping T1656 Impersonation: pretending to be a trusted person to get the victim to act
T1566.004 Phishing: Spearphishing Voice, which MITRE describes as using voice communications, impersonating a trusted source and creating urgency
Examples from MITRE: Scattered Spider impersonated IT staff on phone calls to get remote access tools installed, and Storm-1811 posed as IT support by phone
Supporting technique: T1593 Search Open Websites, gathering voice and video of the target from public sources

1. A Real Case: The Arup Video Call

In early 2024, an employee in the Hong Kong office of Arup, the British engineering firm, joined a video call with people who appeared to be the company's chief financial officer and other colleagues. Believing them to be real, the employee made 15 transfers totalling HK$200 million, around US$25.6 million. CNN reported the total, and Hong Kong broadcaster RTHK, citing police, reported the number of transfers.

Arup later confirmed to CNN that it had reported the incident to Hong Kong police in January 2024 and that fake voices and images had been used.

The striking detail is that a video call was exactly the kind of check that should have made the request safe.

2. How Deepfakes Are Used in Fraud

  • Executive impersonation. A cloned voice or video of a senior leader requests an urgent, confidential payment.
  • IT help desk calls. A caller impersonating an employee asks the help desk to reset a password or MFA, or a caller impersonating IT asks an employee to install a remote access tool or approve a prompt.
  • Family emergency scams. The FBI describes cloned voices of relatives demanding urgent money or a ransom.
  • Fake job candidates. Deepfake video in remote interviews, used to get hired and gain access to company systems.
  • Investment fraud. Fake videos of public figures endorsing a scheme.

3. Can You Spot a Deepfake?

Sometimes. The FBI lists signs to look for: distorted hands or faces, odd teeth or eyes, wrong shadows, lag and unnatural movement in video, and an unusual tone or choice of words in a cloned voice. Asking the person to turn their head, or interrupting with an unexpected question, can expose weaker fakes.

But spotting is not a reliable defence. The technology improves constantly, calls are often short and stressful, and people see what they expect to see. Organisations should assume a convincing fake is possible and build checks that work anyway.

4. Defences That Work Even Against a Perfect Fake

Call back on a number you already have The FBI's advice is to hang up on unexpected calls and contact the person or organisation directly using a number you already trust. A caller cannot fake the person who answers when you ring them back.
  • Agree a secret word or phrase. The FBI recommends this for families. Organisations can use the same idea for urgent requests between finance staff and executives.
  • No payment on a single channel. Require a second, independent confirmation and a second approver for large or unusual transfers, however senior the requester, as in Episode 06.
  • Make "confidential and urgent" a red flag, not a reason to skip the process. Leaders should say publicly that they will never ask anyone to bypass payment checks.
  • Harden the help desk. Do not reset passwords or MFA based on a voice call alone. Require verification through a separate channel, such as a call-back to the number on the HR record or approval from the person's manager.
  • Reduce what attackers can clone. The FBI suggests limiting public photos and voice recordings. For executives, that is not always practical, which is why the process controls above matter more.

5. How Defenders Can Detect It

Deepfake calls often leave little in security logs, but the actions that follow them do. MITRE's detection guidance for voice phishing focuses on correlation:

  • A call followed by unusual activity: a remote management tool installed, a new browser session to an unfamiliar site, or an MFA approval soon after a call from an unknown number.
  • Help desk changes: MFA resets or new devices registered shortly after a phone request, especially for privileged or finance staff.
  • Payment anomalies: new beneficiaries, large transfers outside normal cycles, or several transfers to the same new account in a short period.
  • Staff reports: make it easy and blame-free for employees to report a call that felt wrong, even if they did nothing.

Final Thoughts

Deepfakes do not create a new kind of fraud. They make an old kind, impersonation, far more convincing. The answer is also old: verify requests through a channel the fraudster does not control, and never let urgency or seniority override the process.

Seeing is no longer believing. Calling back is.
