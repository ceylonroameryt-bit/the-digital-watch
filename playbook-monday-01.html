Phishing Triage: A Playbook for the First 15 Minutes

PS
Poorna Sujampathi Rathnayaka
Writer · Cyber Insight · Playbook Monday
Playbook Monday · Ep 01 Incident Response Phishing Email Security Playbook · 10 min read
💬 Feedback
15 min
The window this playbook covers
Confirm, contain and scope before an attacker can make use of what they took
4 layers
In the NCSC's phishing defence model

A message lands in the IT inbox: "I think I clicked something dodgy." What happens in the next quarter of an hour decides whether this is a non-event or the start of a breach. This playbook is written for small and medium-sized teams who need to act fast without a full security operations centre.

Every organisation will have phishing emails get through. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) builds its guidance on that assumption: it describes phishing defence in four layers, and the last one is simply to respond quickly to incidents. CISA, the NSA and the FBI make the same point, recommending a documented incident response plan for phishing and prompt reporting.

This is that plan, in checklist form. It assumes Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, but the steps apply to most email systems. Adapt the tool names to your own.

The person who reports a phish is helping you. Thank them first, every time. Fear of blame is how incidents go unreported.
At a glance Minutes 0–5: Get the facts and the original email
Minutes 5–10: Contain: pull the email, block the link, protect the account
Minutes 10–15: Scope: who else received it, who clicked, what happened next
After 15: Investigate, recover, report and learn

Before You Need It: Three Things to Set Up Now

  • A one-click way to report. A "Report phishing" button in the mail client, and a clear message that reporting is always welcome.
  • Logging that will still be there. Microsoft notes that most Entra ID sign-in and audit data is overwritten after 30 or 90 days, and recommends sending it to a SIEM or long-term store. Check mailbox auditing is switched on too, because Microsoft's phishing playbook depends on it.
  • Permissions in advance. Decide who can search and delete email across all mailboxes, reset passwords and revoke sessions, and make sure they can do so at short notice.

Minutes 0–5: Get the Facts

  1. Get the original email as an attachment, not a forward, so the headers are kept. In Outlook, use "Forward as attachment" or the report button.
  2. Ask the reporter four questions, quickly and without blame:
    • Did you click a link or open an attachment?
    • Did you enter a password, approve an MFA prompt or scan a QR code?
    • Did you download or run anything?
    • Which device were you using, and roughly when?
  3. Look at the email: the real sender address, the link destinations and any attachment names. Do not click anything on your own machine.
  4. Start a timeline. A simple shared note with times and actions. You will need it later.
Escalate immediately if… The user entered credentials and approved MFA, ran a file, has administrator rights, works in finance or payroll, or the email asked for a payment or bank details change. Treat these as a likely compromise and move to containment straight away.

Minutes 5–10: Contain

Pull the email from every mailbox. Search for it by sender, subject or message ID and remove it from all inboxes, so nobody else clicks while you investigate. In Microsoft 365 this is done from the Defender or Purview portals; in Google Workspace, from the security investigation tool.

Block the indicators. Add the sender address or domain, the link domains and any attachment hashes to your email and web blocklists.

If credentials may have been entered, protect the account now, in this order:

  1. Revoke all active sessions and refresh tokens. If the phish used an adversary-in-the-middle kit, the attacker has a session, not just a password (see Threat of the Week, Episode 04).
  2. Reset the password, from a clean device.
  3. Review MFA methods and remove any that were added recently and the user does not recognise.

If a file was run, isolate the device from the network using your endpoint tool, or unplug it from the network and disable Wi-Fi. Do not switch it off if you can avoid it, because memory can hold useful evidence.

Minutes 10–15: Scope

Microsoft's phishing investigation checklist is a good model. In the first pass, answer these:

  • Who else received the email? Use message trace or a content search. Note every recipient.
  • Who clicked? Check URL click data from your email security tool, and web proxy or DNS logs for the link domains.
  • Who signed in afterwards from somewhere unusual? Look at sign-in logs for those recipients in the hours after delivery, especially new IP addresses, countries or devices.
  • Has anything changed in the affected mailboxes? Check for new inbox rules, external forwarding and mailbox delegation. Microsoft specifically highlights newly created rules that forward to external addresses or match words such as "invoice".
  • Were any apps granted access? Check for new OAuth consent grants (see Threat of the Week, Episode 02).

Any recipient who clicked and entered details gets the same containment steps as the original reporter.

After the First 15 Minutes

  • Investigate properly. Review the email headers, the attacker's infrastructure, and what the compromised accounts did. Check sent items for internal phishing from the victim's mailbox.
  • Warn people without causing panic. A short message to staff describing the email and what to do if they clicked usually brings in more reports.
  • Watch for payment fraud. If a finance mailbox was involved, alert the finance team to verify any bank detail changes by phone (see Threat of the Week, Episode 06).
  • Report. In the UK, forward phishing emails to report@phishing.gov.uk. For a significant incident, the NCSC asks organisations to report to it, and personal data breaches may need reporting to the ICO within 72 hours.
  • Learn. Ask why the email got through and why the user trusted it. The answer is often a missing technical control, such as phishing-resistant MFA, rather than a training gap.

Common Mistakes That Cost Time

  • Resetting the password but not revoking sessions. The attacker stays signed in.
  • Investigating one mailbox only. Phishing campaigns rarely target a single person.
  • Clicking the link to "see what it does" from a normal work machine.
  • Blaming the reporter. Next time, they will not tell you.
  • Discovering logs were never switched on, in the middle of the incident.

Final Thoughts

The first fifteen minutes are not about finding out everything. They are about stopping the spread and protecting the accounts that might already be in an attacker's hands. Pull the email, kill the sessions, find the other recipients. The full investigation can follow.

Print this checklist, adapt it to your tools, and run through it once as a practice exercise before you need it for real.

Thank the reporter, pull the email, revoke the sessions, find everyone else who got it. In that order.
